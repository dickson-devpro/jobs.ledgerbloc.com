## Can Farm Workers Get Visa Sponsorship in the UK in 2026?

Farm worker roles in the UK can be sponsored, but the route is different from what most people expect. The majority of these positions are filled through the **Seasonal Worker visa**, a Temporary Worker route that sits entirely outside the Skilled Worker system. If you have seen guides about Skilled Worker sponsorship for agricultural roles, they almost certainly do not apply to picking, planting, packing or general horticultural work. Understanding which route actually applies — and who controls access to it — is the first thing you need to get right before you spend any time or money on applications.

---

## What the Work Actually Involves, and Why Employers Cannot Fill These Roles Locally

Farm work in the UK covers a wider range of tasks than the word "seasonal" suggests. The core of the visa-sponsored market is **horticultural work**: strawberry and berry picking across Kent, Herefordshire, Scotland and Worcestershire; brassica and salad harvesting in Lincolnshire and East Anglia; apple and pear orchards in the South East; and mushroom cultivation in year-round indoor facilities across the Midlands. On top of fieldwork, there are packing-line roles in large cold stores, transplanting and propagation in glasshouses, and irrigation or crop monitoring duties on larger estates.

Some employers also sponsor workers in **poultry processing**, though the allocation for this sector has been more restricted and its continuation year to year is subject to government review. Livestock farms — dairy, pig and sheep units — occasionally seek workers with specific animal husbandry skills, but these tend to recruit locally or through different immigration routes.

The reason UK farms cannot fill these roles domestically is structural and well-documented. The work is physically demanding, often outdoors in variable weather, piece-rate in some cases, and located in rural areas with limited public transport. Domestic workers have broadly moved away from this kind of employment since the expansion of the service sector. Before Brexit, farms relied heavily on EU freedom-of-movement workers, primarily from Romania, Bulgaria and Poland. That labour supply ended in January 2021, and the Seasonal Worker scheme was explicitly designed as a partial replacement. Even with the scheme running, operators and farms regularly report that their allocation is not sufficient to meet peak harvest demand.

The practical consequence for you as an overseas applicant is that **genuine demand exists**, but access is tightly controlled through a small number of licensed scheme operators who distribute placements to farms in their network.

## What Farm Work Pays in the UK

Pay for farm workers is almost always quoted as an **hourly rate**, and the legal floor is the National Living Wage (for workers aged 21 and over) or the relevant National Minimum Wage band for younger workers. These rates are set annually by the government and uprated each April — always check the current figures on the GOV.UK National Minimum Wage page before accepting any offer, because they change.

In practice, indicative pay sits somewhere in the range of **£12 to £15 per hour** for most horticultural roles in 2025, though this figure should be treated as a rough guide. What moves pay up:

- **Piece-rate bonuses**: Many picking roles include a production bonus on top of the base rate, meaning fast, experienced pickers can earn noticeably more than slower ones.
- **Specialist skills**: Tractor or forklift operation, pesticide handling, or supervisory duties all attract higher rates.
- **Employer and crop**: Larger corporate farming operations and glasshouse growers often pay more consistently than smaller family farms.
- **Region**: Labour markets differ across the country. Operators in regions with higher living costs sometimes adjust rates accordingly.

Most scheme operators provide accommodation on or near the farm, which is typically charged to the worker at a set weekly rate deducted from wages. This is legal, but you should get the deduction amount in writing before you accept a placement — and verify it does not reduce your effective hourly pay below the minimum wage, which is a legal requirement.

## The Visa Route in Detail

### The Seasonal Worker Visa (Temporary Worker Route)

The Seasonal Worker visa sits under the **Temporary Worker** immigration category in the UK points-based system. It is not the Skilled Worker route, and it does not lead to settlement or indefinite leave to remain. This is important to understand from the outset.

**Who initiates it:** The process begins with the **scheme operator**, not the individual farm and not you. The Home Office licenses a small number of approved operators who are authorised to recruit overseas workers and issue Certificates of Sponsorship (CoS) for horticultural and poultry sector roles. These operators work with farms across the country and place workers according to seasonal demand.

**What the employer (scheme operator) must do:**
1. Hold a current Home Office sponsor licence for the Seasonal Worker route.
2. Have a valid allocation of places for the season (the government sets an annual quota; check GOV.UK for the current figure).
3. Issue you a Certificate of Sponsorship with a specific reference number.
4. Ensure the role and pay comply with the requirements of the route.

**What you must provide:**
- A valid passport.
- The Certificate of Sponsorship reference number from the licensed operator.
- Evidence that you can support yourself financially during your stay (unless the operator's offer letter satisfies this requirement — check the current rules).
- A completed online visa application and, depending on your nationality, biometric enrolment at a visa application centre.
- The visa application fee and the Immigration Health Surcharge — verify current amounts on GOV.UK as both change.

The visa application is made online through the GOV.UK website. You do not apply through the farm, through an agent, or through a third-party website. If someone is directing you to apply anywhere other than the official GOV.UK application portal, treat that as a serious warning sign.

## Qualifications and Licences Specific to This Role in the UK

For general picking, packing and planting roles, **there is no formal qualification requirement**. The Seasonal Worker visa does not have a minimum education or skills threshold in the way the Skilled Worker route does.

However, some roles within farm work do carry specific requirements:

- **Pesticide application**: Any worker who applies pesticides professionally in the UK must hold a Certificate of Competence issued under the National Professions Certificate (NPC) scheme, or an equivalent qualification recognised by the chemicals regulator. If the job advert mentions spraying or crop protection duties, ask whether your home-country qualification is recognised or whether you will need to complete UK training.
- **Driving and machinery**: Operating tractors, telehandlers or forklifts on a public road requires a valid driving licence and, for forklifts, a recognised operator certificate. A non-UK driving licence may be valid for some time after arrival, but the rules depend on your country of origin. If you are also considering HGV driving roles in the UK, our guide on [Truck Driver Jobs in the UK with Visa Sponsorship 2026](/truck-driver-jobs-uk-visa-sponsorship/) covers licence conversion in detail.
- **Food safety**: Workers in packing facilities may be required to complete a basic food hygiene certificate, which is typically a short online course that employers arrange on arrival.

No professional registration body governs general farm work in the UK in the way the NMC governs nursing, so there is no pre-arrival registration step for most applicants.

## Which Employers Actually Sponsor Farm Workers, and How to Find Them

The critical distinction in this sector is that **individual farms do not hold Seasonal Worker visa sponsorship licences** in most cases. Sponsorship runs through **licensed scheme operators**, of which there are currently a small number approved by the Home Office. These operators act as intermediaries between overseas workers and the farms. Examples of operator types (not an exhaustive list of current operators, which you should verify on GOV.UK) include:

| Operator type | How they recruit | Farms they serve |
|---|---|---|
| Large agri-recruitment companies | Online portals, overseas recruitment drives | Berry, brassica, salad, glasshouse operators |
| Specialist horticulture co-operatives | Agent networks in source countries | Orchard and soft fruit farms |
| Poultry sector operators | Direct recruitment via industry contacts | Processing plants, seasonal poultry farms |

To find legitimate opportunities, go to the **GOV.UK register of licensed sponsors** and filter for Seasonal Worker route operators. This list is the authoritative source. You can then apply directly through each operator's own recruitment portal or website.

Large farming businesses such as commercial berry growers in Scotland and Herefordshire, salad producers in Lincolnshire, and glasshouse tomato and cucumber growers in the Midlands and East Anglia are among the types of operation that regularly take placements through these schemes. None of them will sponsor you directly — they work through the operator.

## Common Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected

1. **Applying directly to a farm rather than a scheme operator.** The farm cannot issue your Certificate of Sponsorship. Fix: identify the scheme operator the farm uses, or apply through the operator's own portal.

2. **Accepting a CoS reference number from an unlicensed operator.** A fake CoS will result in visa refusal and may flag your record. Fix: check the GOV.UK sponsor register before submitting your application.

3. **Submitting financial evidence that does not meet current thresholds.** The rules on how much you need to show in savings change. Fix: check GOV.UK for the current maintenance requirement at the time of your application.

4. **Using an expired passport.** Your passport must remain valid for the duration of your intended stay. Fix: renew before applying and allow time for passport processing in your home country.

5. **Misrepresenting employment history or income on the application.** Any inconsistency between your application and supporting documents can lead to refusal and future bans. Fix: be accurate and consistent throughout.

6. **Not accounting for Immigration Health Surcharge costs.** Many applicants are surprised by this charge at the payment stage. Fix: use the GOV.UK IHS calculator before you start the application.

7. **Assuming the visa can be extended or switched.** The Seasonal Worker visa cannot be extended beyond the permitted duration and generally cannot be switched to another route from inside the UK. Fix: plan your return travel in advance and do not overstay.

8. **Relying on third-party websites for the application itself.** Several unofficial websites mimic the GOV.UK application process and charge fees for a free service. Fix: the correct URL is gov.uk/apply-to-come-to-the-uk — bookmark it and use it directly.

## Realistic Timeline: From Application to Arrival

The timeline below is indicative and assumes you are applying from outside the UK with a straightforward case.

| Stage | Typical timeframe |
|---|---|
| Scheme operator application and placement offer | 2–8 weeks depending on season and demand |
| Certificate of Sponsorship issued by operator | Within a few days of placement confirmation |
| Online visa application completed and submitted | 1–3 days once you have the CoS reference |
| Biometric appointment at visa application centre | 1–2 weeks for an appointment in most countries |
| Home Office decision (standard service) | Around 3 weeks from biometrics, but variable |
| Travel and arrival | Typically 1–2 days after visa issued |
| **Total from first application to starting work** | **6–14 weeks in most cases** |

Priority processing is available in some countries for an additional fee, but it does not guarantee a different outcome — it only speeds up the decision. Always build in time for delays, particularly at the start of a harvest season when operators are processing large volumes of applications simultaneously.

## Scam Warning: How to Protect Yourself

Farm work visa scams are among the most common in the UK immigration space. The pattern is consistent: a recruiter — often operating via WhatsApp, Facebook or a professional-looking website — claims to have direct access to UK farm jobs and offers to secure your visa in exchange for a fee. The fee is paid, the CoS never arrives, and the "recruiter" disappears.

**The rules are simple:**

- **No legitimate employer or scheme operator will charge you a fee to apply for a job or to receive a Certificate of Sponsorship.** If anyone asks you to pay for a CoS, for a job placement, or for "visa processing" on their behalf, stop contact immediately.
- **Only scheme operators on the GOV.UK register can issue Certificates of Sponsorship** for the Seasonal Worker route. Verify any operator before engaging with them.
- **Visa fees are paid directly to the Home Office** through the GOV.UK application portal. No third party should be collecting money for the visa itself.
- **There is no such thing as a "guaranteed" visa.** Anyone who claims to guarantee your visa approval is lying to you.

If you encounter a suspected scam operation, you can report it to Action Fraud in the UK and to your home country's consumer protection authority.

## Is This Route Right for You?

The Seasonal Worker visa is a genuine, well-established route that places tens of thousands of overseas workers on UK farms each year. It is not a pathway to settlement, it does not allow your family to join you, and it requires you to find your way through a small number of licensed gatekeepers. But for someone who wants to earn in sterling, gain UK work experience, and potentially return in subsequent seasons, it is a real and functioning option.

If your longer-term goal is a route that leads to settlement or that allows dependants, you will need to look at roles that qualify under the Skilled Worker route — which typically requires degree-level or equivalent skills, a sponsored job offer at or above the relevant salary threshold, and an employer with a Skilled Worker sponsor licence. Most standard farm worker roles do not currently meet those criteria, though agricultural management, agronomist and specialist technical roles sometimes do.
