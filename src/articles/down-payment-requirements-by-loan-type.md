**The minimum down payment you need depends entirely on which loan programme you use: FHA asks for as little as 3.5%, conventional programmes can go as low as 3%, while VA and USDA loans require nothing down at all for those who qualify. Jumbo loans sit at the other end of the spectrum, with many lenders expecting 10% to 20% or more.** Understanding these thresholds before you start house-hunting tells you how much you actually need to save — and whether a programme change could get you into a home sooner.

## Why Loan Type Matters More Than Most Buyers Realise

Most buyers focus on interest rates and monthly payments. The down payment requirement, though, shapes almost everything else: how long you need to save, whether you pay mortgage insurance, what your loan-to-value ratio looks like on day one, and even which lenders will compete for your business.

Each loan programme is backed by a different authority with its own risk appetite. Loans backed by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) are insured by the US government through HUD, which lets lenders accept smaller down payments and lower credit scores. Loans sold to Fannie Mae (the Federal National Mortgage Association) or Freddie Mac (the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation) follow their own published guidelines. VA loans are guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs. USDA loans carry a guarantee from the US Department of Agriculture. Jumbo loans carry no government backing at all, so individual lenders set their own rules.

Knowing which bucket your situation falls into is the starting point for every other calculation.

## Conventional Loans: What Down Payment Do They Require?

### The Standard Conventional Loan

A conventional loan is simply one that is not backed by a federal government agency. To be sold in the secondary market, it must conform to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac guidelines and fall within the FHFA's annual conforming loan limits. The standard down payment associated with conventional loans in the broader market is 20%, because at that level you avoid private mortgage insurance entirely. However, 20% has not been a legal minimum for decades.

### The 3% Minimum: HomeReady, Home Possible and Conventional 97

Fannie Mae's HomeReady and Freddie Mac's Home Possible programmes both allow 3% down payments for qualifying borrowers. The standard Conventional 97 product also permits 3% down. These programmes are aimed at low-to-moderate income borrowers, and income limits apply in many cases — verify the current thresholds on FannieMae.com and FreddieMac.com, as they are updated at least annually.

Putting down less than 20% on a conventional loan triggers PMI, which protects the lender rather than the borrower. PMI can be cancelled once the loan-to-value ratio reaches 80%, unlike FHA mortgage insurance which behaves differently. For a detailed breakdown of how PMI and mortgage insurance premiums compare, see our guide to [Mortgage Insurance Premium and PMI Explained 2026](/mortgage-insurance-premium-pmi-explained/).

### Illustrative Example: Conventional Loan at 5% Down

Suppose a borrower is purchasing a property at an illustrative price of $350,000 and puts down 5% ($17,500). The loan amount would be $332,500. Because the LTV is 95%, PMI would apply. At an illustrative PMI rate of 0.6% per year (rates vary significantly by lender, score and LTV), the monthly PMI cost would be roughly $166. Once the loan balance drops to $280,000 — 80% of the original value — the borrower can request PMI cancellation under the Homeowners Protection Act. These figures are illustrative; actual rates depend on lender pricing, credit score and market conditions at the time of application.

## FHA Loans: Lower Bar, But Not Free

### The 3.5% Threshold and the 580 Score Floor

FHA-insured loans, administered through the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), are a popular choice for first-time buyers because of their relatively accessible requirements. The programme currently allows a 3.5% down payment for borrowers with a FICO credit score of 580 or above. Borrowers with scores between 500 and 579 are permitted to use FHA financing but must put down at least 10%.

Scores below 500 are generally ineligible under FHA programme guidelines, though individual lenders may impose stricter overlays — meaning their internal minimums may be higher than FHA's published floor.

### Mortgage Insurance Is Permanent on Most FHA Loans

The critical trade-off with FHA financing is mortgage insurance. FHA loans carry both an upfront mortgage insurance premium (UFMIP) — typically 1.75% of the base loan amount, financed into the loan — and an annual MIP charged monthly. For loans where the down payment is below 10%, MIP runs for the life of the loan. This is a meaningful long-term cost that deserves careful modelling against the alternative of a conventional loan with PMI that can be cancelled.

Current MIP rates and the exact conditions under which they apply should be verified directly on HUD.gov, as the agency adjusts them periodically. HUD announced a reduction in annual MIP rates in 2023, and the rates in effect in 2026 should be confirmed before any financial projections are made.

### Illustrative Example: FHA at 3.5% Down

At an illustrative purchase price of $300,000, a 3.5% down payment is $10,500, leaving a base loan of $289,500. The UFMIP at 1.75% adds approximately $5,066, bringing the financed amount to around $294,566. Monthly MIP at an illustrative annual rate of 0.55% on the loan balance would add approximately $135 per month initially. These are illustrative figures; your actual costs will differ based on current HUD MIP schedules, the term you choose, and your lender's pricing.

## VA Loans: Zero Down for Eligible Borrowers

### Who Qualifies for a VA Loan

VA loans are guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs and are available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, National Guard and Reserve members who meet minimum service requirements, and surviving spouses of veterans who died in service or from a service-connected disability. Eligibility is established through a Certificate of Eligibility (COE), which can be obtained through the VA's eBenefits portal, directly through a VA-approved lender, or by submitting VA Form 26-1880.

There is no minimum down payment required on a VA loan, and there is no PMI. For an in-depth look at the full programme, including entitlement rules and funding fees, see our [VA Home Loan Eligibility and Benefits: 2026 Guide](/va-home-loan-eligibility-and-benefits/).

### The VA Funding Fee

In place of mortgage insurance, VA loans carry a one-time funding fee paid to the VA. The fee percentage varies based on the type of service, whether it is a first or subsequent use of the benefit, and the down payment amount chosen. Borrowers who receive VA disability compensation may be exempt from the funding fee entirely. Current funding fee tables are published on VA.gov and should be verified before any financial projections are made.

### Illustrative Example: VA Loan With Zero Down

On a $400,000 purchase with zero down payment and a first-use funding fee of 2.15% (illustrative — verify on VA.gov), the fee would be $8,600. If financed into the loan, the total loan amount becomes $408,600. There is no PMI. Over a 30-year term, the absence of PMI can represent tens of thousands of dollars in savings compared with a conventional loan at the same LTV, depending on the rate environment.

## USDA Loans: Zero Down in Eligible Rural Areas

### Programme Basics

The USDA's Single Family Housing Guaranteed Loan Programme offers zero down payment financing for properties located in eligible rural and some suburban areas. Eligibility is determined by both the property location and the borrower's household income, which must not exceed 115% of the area median income (AMI) for the relevant county. The USDA's online eligibility map tool allows borrowers to check whether a specific address qualifies.

Loans are made by approved private lenders and guaranteed by the USDA, meaning the agency covers a portion of the loss if the borrower defaults. This allows lenders to offer competitive rates without requiring a down payment.

### Guarantee Fee Structure

Like VA loans, USDA loans use a guarantee fee structure rather than PMI. There is an upfront guarantee fee — currently 1% of the loan amount — and an annual fee of 0.35% of the remaining loan balance, charged monthly. These rates have been relatively stable in recent years but should be verified on USDA.gov before any projections are made, as they are set annually through the appropriations process.

## Jumbo Loans: Higher Stakes, Higher Requirements

### What Makes a Loan Jumbo

A jumbo loan is any mortgage that exceeds the conforming loan limit for the county where the property sits. The FHFA announces updated conforming loan limits each November, effective from the following year. In most of the continental United States, the baseline limit is a specific figure (verify the current year's limit at FHFA.gov); in high-cost counties — parts of California, New York, Hawaii and others — the limit is considerably higher.

Because jumbo loans cannot be purchased by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, lenders hold them on their own balance sheets or sell them in private markets. This creates genuine risk concentration for the lender.

### What Down Payments Lenders Typically Require

Most lenders require between 10% and 20% down on a jumbo loan, with 20% being common for loan amounts significantly above the conforming limit. Some lenders offer 10% down jumbo products to borrowers with very strong credit profiles — typically FICO scores above 720 to 740 — and substantial liquid reserves after closing. Reserves of six to twelve months of mortgage payments are a common requirement.

Jumbo guidelines are lender-specific and change with market conditions. There is no agency standard to fall back on. If you are shopping for a jumbo loan, comparing multiple lender term sheets is especially important.

### Illustrative Example: Jumbo at 15% Down

On an illustrative purchase of $1,200,000 in a county where the conforming limit is $766,550 (illustrative — verify current limit on FHFA.gov), a 15% down payment is $180,000, leaving a loan of $1,020,000. At 15% down, some lenders will charge a higher rate or require private jumbo mortgage insurance. A 20% down payment ($240,000) leaves a loan of $960,000 and typically secures better pricing and avoids insurance requirements. The interest rate differential between 15% and 20% down can be meaningful over the life of a large loan.

## How Does Credit Score Interact With Down Payment?

Credit score and down payment are the two levers lenders use to assess risk, and they interact. On an FHA loan, the score determines which minimum down payment applies. On conventional loans, the score does not change the headline minimum (3%), but a lower score raises the cost of PMI and typically the interest rate itself. On jumbo loans, a score below lender thresholds can make a product unavailable regardless of down payment size.

## Down Payment Sources: What Lenders Will and Will Not Accept

### Acceptable Sources

Most programmes accept down payment funds from the following sources, subject to documentation requirements:

- Personal savings in bank or investment accounts (typically requiring two months of bank statements)
- Gift funds from family members or approved organisations, with a signed gift letter confirming no repayment is expected
- Proceeds from the sale of another property
- Employer assistance programmes
- Down payment assistance (DPA) grants or second mortgages from state and local housing finance agencies

### Sources That Raise Red Flags

Lenders will scrutinise large unexplained deposits in your bank statements during the look-back period. Funds borrowed from a personal loan or credit card — effectively leveraging the down payment — are generally not acceptable because they add to your debt obligations and are not considered the borrower's own assets. Lenders will ask about the source of any large deposit, so preparing documentation in advance is essential.

## 6 Common Down Payment Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

1. **Assuming 20% is always required.** It is not, and waiting to save to that level costs some buyers years. Review all programme options — you may qualify for 3% or 3.5% down today. *Fix:* Request a loan programme comparison from at least two lenders showing options at different down payment levels.

2. **Forgetting closing costs.** Down payment is not the only cash you need at settlement. Closing costs typically run 2%–5% of the loan amount and cover lender fees, title insurance, prepaid taxes and insurance, and other items. *Fix:* Ask each lender for a Loan Estimate (LE) — a standardised form required under RESPA — within three business days of application. This shows projected closing costs alongside the loan terms.

3. **Moving large sums of money shortly before applying.** Unexplained deposits create underwriting delays. *Fix:* Keep funds in the same accounts for at least 60 days before application. If you need to consolidate savings, do it earlier.

4. **Relying on an FHA loan without modelling the long-term MIP cost.** For borrowers who qualify for conventional financing at a similar rate, the life-of-loan MIP on FHA can exceed the savings from the lower down payment over a 10-year horizon. *Fix:* Ask your loan officer to model the total five-year and ten-year cost of each option side by side.

5. **Not checking for down payment assistance.** Many buyers on moderate incomes qualify for state or local DPA grants or forgivable second mortgages that they never investigate. *Fix:* Use HUD's approved housing counsellor directory (available on HUD.gov) to find a counsellor who knows your local programmes.

6. **Choosing a loan type based on rate alone without considering mortgage insurance.** A slightly lower rate on an FHA loan can be more than offset by mandatory MIP, especially if you expect to hold the loan for a long time. *Fix:* Always compare the annual percentage rate (APR) and the projected total interest and insurance paid over your likely holding period, not just the headline rate.

## Should You Put More Down Than the Minimum?

There is no universal answer. A larger down payment reduces your loan balance, cuts monthly payments, may eliminate or reduce mortgage insurance, and typically secures a better interest rate tier. On the other side of the ledger, it reduces your liquid reserves, limits your ability to diversify into other investments, and delays your purchase if you are still saving.

The right answer depends on your income stability, emergency fund, local market conditions, and the specific loan pricing on offer. These are exactly the variables a mortgage broker or licensed loan officer should help you model — ideally alongside an independent financial adviser who has visibility into your full financial picture.

What is clear from the data is that the minimum down payment is a floor, not a target. Borrowers who can comfortably exceed the minimum while maintaining healthy liquid reserves are generally in a stronger financial position at closing.
