*Checked 11 November 2026. Rules and fees change; confirm against the official source for your route.*

There is no application form called immigration. There are specific routes, each with its own eligibility rules, evidence, fee and queue, and choosing the wrong one is the most expensive mistake available at the start of the process.

Fees are almost never refunded on refusal. A refusal stays on your record and must be declared in every future application anywhere in the world. And the lost time is usually the larger cost, because several routes score you on age.

## Four questions that decide your route

**What is your basis?** Work, study, family, business or investment, protection, or ancestry. People frequently qualify on more than one and never check — someone with an Irish grandparent and a job offer in Dublin usually has a far easier path through citizenship by descent than through work sponsorship.

**Temporary or permanent?** Temporary permission is faster and cheaper but conditional, expiring and usually tied to an employer. Ask of any route: does this lead anywhere, and after how long?

**Does it need a sponsor?** Employer-led systems like the UK require a licensed employer to offer a specific job before you can apply. Points-based systems like Australia and Canada let you apply on your own merits. Cap-and-lottery systems like the US H-1B require an employer *and* luck. If you are in an employer-led system your project is a job search; in a points system it is a documentation exercise. Confusing them wastes months.

**What is the money test?** Almost every route has one and it is rarely the application fee. Work routes set a salary floor; study routes require tuition and living costs; family routes set a sponsor income; investment routes set a capital minimum. And nearly all require a maintenance balance held for a defined period.

## The money test by route type

| Route | Typical money test |
|---|---|
| Work, sponsored | A salary floor, plus maintenance unless the employer certifies it |
| Study | First-year tuition plus living costs for a set number of months |
| Family | A minimum sponsor income, evidenced over a defined period |
| Investment | A minimum capital sum, plus proof of lawful source |
| Points-based | Settlement funds, scaled by family size |

The pattern is identical everywhere: not the balance today, but the balance held continuously for a defined window, evidenced in the specified format. Statements printed from a banking app are frequently rejected where stamped statements are required.

## The sequence

**1. Confirm eligibility against the official source.** The government page, not a blog or a forum. Rules change and most published material lags.

**2. Get credentials recognised.** Skills assessment, qualification equivalency, professional registration. Slow, and they block everything downstream.

**3. Sit your language test.** Book early. If your score sits near a band boundary, sit it again.

**4. Gather documents, slowest first.** Police certificates, civil documents, employment references, bank statements.

**5. Satisfy the money test and start the clock.** The money must be in place before the holding period starts.

**6. Apply** — once, completely, with everything attached.

**7. Biometrics, medicals, interview**, as required.

**8. Decision, then travel.**

## The document pack, by how long it takes

**Police certificates.** From every country where you have lived for a qualifying period, usually six or twelve months, since a defined age. Some issue in days; others take months and require application in person or through a consulate.

**Skills assessment or qualification recognition.** Months, routinely, with at least one request for further evidence partway through.

**Civil documents.** Birth certificate, marriage certificate, divorce decree, adoption papers. Some systems require long-form versions. Many require legalisation — an apostille where both countries are party to the Hague Convention, consular legalisation where not.

**Employment evidence.** References on letterhead in a specified format stating title, exact dates, duties and hours, plus payslips, tax records and contracts. The hard ones are from employers that no longer exist, where the answer is usually tax records plus a statutory declaration.

**Financial evidence.** Statements covering the required period in the required format.

**Passport.** Check the expiry against your whole timeline, not against today.

## Where applications fail

**Inconsistency across your own documents.** Your reference says March, your CV says April, your tax record says February. An officer who finds three dates has a reason to doubt everything else.

**Non-disclosure.** A declared old refusal is a fact to be explained. An undeclared one, found later, is deception — and deception findings carry bans measured in years.

**Translations.** Documents not in the official language usually need certified translations in a specified format with translator details.

**Wrong form, wrong route.** Fees are not transferable.

**Expiry mid-process.** A passport, a language certificate, a skills assessment.

## When you need a regulated adviser

You do not need one for a straightforward application. Many people complete points-based and study applications themselves without difficulty.

You very much should take advice in these six situations:

**You have a previous refusal.** Particularly one involving any allegation about the accuracy of what you said. The remedy depends on the exact ground, and getting it wrong compounds the problem.

**You have a criminal record of any kind**, including matters that did not result in a conviction, cautions, and matters that are spent at home but may not be treated as spent abroad.

**Your family situation is complex.** Children from a previous relationship, a partner with their own status issues, a dependant approaching an age-out date, or a custody arrangement affecting whether a child can travel.

**You are on a business or investment route.** The documentation is a drafting exercise, not a collection one, and the sums at stake dwarf the fee.

**There is a medical issue that might engage a health requirement.** Particularly where the requirement applies to the whole family unit including non-migrating members, as it does in Australia.

**You have overstayed, worked without permission, or been removed from a country.** These carry consequences that are not obvious from the published rules.

## How to check an adviser is real

Most countries regulate immigration advice and maintain a public register. In the UK, advisers must be regulated; in Australia, registered migration agents appear on a public register; the United States restricts representation to licensed attorneys and accredited representatives.

Three warnings that apply everywhere:

**Nobody can guarantee an outcome.** A guarantee is a lie and the clearest single signal of a scam.

**Nobody should ask you to sign a blank form.** You are legally responsible for every statement in your application, including ones an adviser typed.

**Money flows from you to a regulated adviser for advice, and never to an employer for a job.** Any request to pay for sponsorship itself is fraudulent.

## If you are refused

The refusal letter should state the ground and the rule relied on, and the remedy depends entirely on which ground.

**A simple evidential gap** is usually best fixed by a fresh application rather than a challenge.

**An error of law or fact** may be reviewable. Many systems offer internal administrative review for certain decision types with deadlines measured in days. Some decisions carry an appeal right to a tribunal.

**A deception finding** is a different category entirely, carries multi-year bans, and should not be addressed without specialist advice.

Deadlines for review are short and strictly applied. Do not lodge a fresh application while a review of the same decision is pending without advice. And disclose the refusal in every future application anywhere, because authorities increasingly share information.

## Variations that catch people out

**Citizenship by birth.** Some countries grant it to anyone born on their territory; most do not. The UK has not had unconditional birthright citizenship since 1983.

**Dependant work rights.** In some systems a dependent partner can work freely; in others not at all. This can halve a household's income.

**Children ageing out.** Dependent children usually lose derivative status at 18 or 21, and the date can arrive mid-process.

**Absence limits.** Routes leading to permanent status cap time spent outside the country during the qualifying period.

**Public funds conditions.** Many temporary statuses prohibit claiming certain benefits, and claiming them in error can jeopardise your status even in good faith.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I apply without a job offer?**
In points-based systems yes. In employer-led systems generally no.

**Which country is easiest?**
No general answer. It depends on your occupation, age, language, qualifications and family connections.

**Will a refusal ruin my chances?**
Not necessarily. A refusal on eligibility grounds is a fact to declare. One involving deception is far more serious.

**Can I travel while a decision is pending?**
Often not, particularly where your passport has been submitted.

**Does time on a tourist visa count towards settlement?**
Almost never.

**What is an apostille?**
A certificate attached to a public document confirming it is genuine, used between Hague Convention countries.

**Do I have to use the government's own form?**
Yes. Third-party sites charging to "complete your application" are reselling access to a free form.

**How much does a regulated adviser cost?**
It varies enormously by jurisdiction and complexity. Get a written fee agreement covering what is included and what triggers additional charges before you engage anyone.

## What happens after approval

The decision is not the end, and the steps afterwards carry their own deadlines.

**Entry window.** Many visas must be activated by a first entry before a stated date, or they lapse. People book flights around a start date and discover the visa expired before they used it.

**Residence documentation.** Several countries issue a card or digital record after arrival, with a deadline to collect or activate it.

**Local registration.** Some countries require you to register with local authorities or police within days of arriving, and the penalty for missing it is out of proportion to the effort of doing it.

**Tax and social security numbers.** Usually applied for after arrival and usually needed before you can be paid correctly. Without one, many countries withhold tax at the highest marginal rate until you fix it.

**Read your conditions.** Work restrictions, study restrictions, geographic conditions, no-recourse-to-public-funds conditions. Breaching one unknowingly is still breaching it, and "nobody told me" is not a defence anywhere.

## A document tracker that actually works

The people who find this process manageable all do some version of the same thing: one list, kept current.

| Column | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Document | What it is |
| Who it is for | Main applicant, partner, each child |
| Issuing body | With its website, so you can chase |
| Requested on | So you know when to follow up |
| Received on | So you can see what is outstanding |
| Expires | The column that saves applications |
| Translation needed | Yes or no, and whether it is done |
| Legalisation needed | Apostille, consular, or none |

A family application with four people and three previous countries of residence generates forty or fifty rows. Nobody holds that reliably in memory across nine months, and the failures are almost always a row nobody was looking at.

## Common mistakes that are entirely avoidable

**Starting the fast steps first.** The language test takes a fortnight; the police certificate from a country you left in 2014 takes three months. People sit the test in week one and start the certificate in week twelve.

**Treating the online form as the application.** The form is the index. The evidence is the application.

**Submitting incomplete to meet a deadline.** An incomplete application is not a placeholder; it is usually decided on what was submitted.

**Assuming the adviser is checking.** You are legally responsible for every statement, including ones somebody else typed.

**Not keeping copies.** Keep a complete copy of everything you submit, in the order you submitted it. If a decision is challenged, reconstructing the file from memory is close to impossible.

## Who to ask when you are stuck

**The official helpline or enquiry service for your route.** Slow, but authoritative, and the answer is on the record.

**The assessing authority or professional body**, for anything about qualifications. They answer qualification questions better than immigration departments do.

**A regulated adviser**, for anything in the six situations above.

**Not** a forum, not a social media group, and not an agent who contacted you first. Forums are full of people confidently describing rules that changed two years ago, and the confident ones are frequently the most out of date.

**What it costs.** Fees vary enormously by jurisdiction and complexity. Get a written fee agreement covering what is included, what triggers additional charges, and what happens if the application is refused, before you engage anyone.
