**The technology sector accounts for a substantial share of all skilled worker visa sponsorships issued in the UK, United States, Canada, Germany, and Australia every year — and for good reason: software engineers, data scientists, cloud architects, and cybersecurity specialists remain in short supply in nearly every major economy. If you hold demonstrable technical skills and are prepared to run a focused, informed job search, sponsored tech roles are among the most accessible skilled migration pathways available to international candidates in 2026.**

## Who This Guide Is For

This guide is written for **job seekers** — software developers, data professionals, DevOps engineers, product managers, and other technology workers who want to find an employer willing to sponsor their visa. It covers the UK Skilled Worker route in most depth, with substantive sections on the US, Canada, Germany, and Australia, because those are the markets where sponsored tech hiring is both documented and structurally significant.

If you are an employer looking to sponsor tech hires, this guide will give you useful context, but you will find the operational detail you need in our employer-focused pieces, including [How to Apply for a UK Skilled Worker Sponsor Licence](/how-to-apply-uk-skilled-worker-sponsor-licence/) and [How to Sponsor an Employee for a Work Visa: Step by Step](/how-to-sponsor-employee-work-visa-step-by-step/).

## Why Tech Is the Sector Most Likely to Sponsor

Sponsorship is not free for employers. In the UK, a medium or large business pays the Immigration Skills Charge on top of the visa application and legal fees — at current indicative rates, around £1,000 per year of sponsorship for larger sponsors, meaning a five-year Skilled Worker visa costs roughly £5,000 in skills charge alone, before any immigration solicitor or Home Office application fees are added. (Verify the current fee schedule at [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skilled-worker-visa-immigration-skills-charge) — fees change.) Despite this, tech employers pay it routinely, for a simple reason: the cost of leaving a senior engineering role unfilled, or the opportunity cost of delaying a product launch, typically exceeds the sponsorship overhead many times over.

The result is that software and technology firms make up a disproportionate share of the UK's Register of Licensed Sponsors — the publicly searchable database maintained by the Home Office that lists every organisation approved to issue a Certificate of Sponsorship (COS). In the US, tech companies consistently dominate the annual H-1B employer data published by USCIS. In Canada's Global Talent Stream, software occupations sit at the centre of the eligible job list. This is not anecdote; it is structural, and it is why a well-targeted tech job search can yield genuine sponsorship results.

## Which Types of Tech Firms Actually Sponsor?

Not all technology companies sponsor with equal frequency. Understanding the landscape helps you direct your effort where it is most likely to pay off.

### Large Technology Enterprises

The largest global technology companies — cloud platform providers, enterprise software firms, semiconductor manufacturers, and major internet platforms — maintain dedicated immigration programmes staffed by internal immigration teams or retained corporate immigration firms. They sponsor at scale, across multiple countries, and typically have defined processes. For an international candidate this means more transparency (job postings often state "visa sponsorship available") but also intense competition.

### Mid-Sized Product and SaaS Companies

Software-as-a-Service businesses with headcounts in the hundreds to low thousands are often overlooked by international candidates fixated on brand-name employers, but they are frequently active sponsors. They have grown beyond the informal startup phase, have HR processes in place, and are hungry for specialist talent in areas such as backend engineering, machine learning, and cloud infrastructure. Sponsorship is not always advertised prominently, which is precisely why knowing how to ask matters — see [How to Ask an Employer About Visa Sponsorship (2026)](/how-to-ask-employer-about-visa-sponsorship/) for a practical script.

### IT Consultancies and Managed Service Providers

Large IT consultancies — including the major global professional services firms and specialist technology consultancies — are among the highest-volume sponsors in the UK in particular. They hire across disciplines (developers, architects, analysts, project managers) and often have established sponsor licence infrastructure. The caveat: some consultancy models involve secondment or client-site working, which can raise compliance questions around third-party working arrangements under UK sponsor licence rules. This is a legitimate area to clarify with the employer or your own immigration adviser before accepting an offer.

### Defence Technology and Government Contractors

This segment sponsors less visibly, partly because roles frequently require security clearances that are harder to obtain for candidates without existing residency rights. Clearance-dependent roles are generally not viable for new sponsored hires. However, these firms also employ significant numbers of software engineers in non-classified roles, and some do sponsor for those positions.

### Startups and Scale-Ups

Early-stage startups rarely hold a sponsor licence, but they may apply for one to secure a specific hire if the business case is strong enough. UK startups may also explore the Innovator Founder visa if a candidate is establishing a business rather than filling an employment role. Scale-ups — typically Series B and beyond — are more likely to have sponsorship infrastructure in place. If you are targeting a startup, checking the Home Office register (see [How to Check If a Company Has a UK Sponsor Licence](/how-to-check-if-company-holds-sponsor-licence/)) tells you immediately whether they can move quickly or would need to build from scratch.

## Country-by-Country: The Main Visa Routes for Sponsored Tech Workers

### United Kingdom — Skilled Worker Visa

The UK's Skilled Worker visa is the primary route for sponsored tech employment. To qualify:

- The role must appear on the eligible occupation list (most software, IT, and data roles do — check the current list at gov.uk/skilled-worker-visa).
- The employer must hold a valid sponsor licence issued by the Home Office.
- The salary must meet the general threshold (currently £38,700 gross per year, indicative — verify at gov.uk) or the going rate for the occupation code, whichever is higher.
- The employer must assign a Certificate of Sponsorship (COS) — a unique reference number, not a physical document, that forms the basis of the visa application.

Once a COS is assigned, the candidate applies online, pays the visa application fee and the Immigration Health Surcharge, and submits biometrics. Standard processing from outside the UK currently runs approximately three weeks, though priority services are available (check gov.uk for current availability and fees).

For a fuller explanation of the COS mechanics, see [Certificate of Sponsorship Explained for Employers 2026](/certificate-of-sponsorship-explained-employers/).

**Illustrative worked example (UK):**
Priya is a software engineer based in India with six years of backend experience. A UK SaaS company with an existing sponsor licence offers her a role as a Senior Software Developer at £52,000 per year. The salary exceeds both the general threshold and the going rate for the relevant occupation code. The employer assigns a Restricted COS (illustratively — confirm the current COS type rules at gov.uk), pays the Immigration Skills Charge (£1,000 per year for a large employer, so approximately £5,000 for a five-year grant), and Priya applies for her Skilled Worker visa from India. This is illustrative: actual costs, timelines, and eligibility must be confirmed against current Home Office guidance and, where appropriate, with a licensed immigration adviser.

### United States — H-1B and Other Routes

The H-1B is the dominant employer-sponsored visa for speciality occupation workers, which covers almost all professional software and technology roles. Key features:

- Employers file a **Form I-129** (Petition for Nonimmigrant Worker) with USCIS.
- Most private employers are subject to the annual cap (currently 65,000 regular cap plus 20,000 for US master's degree holders) and an electronic lottery, typically held in March for an October 1 start.
- Cap-exempt employers (universities, certain non-profits, government research) can file at any time.
- The **Labor Condition Application (LCA)** must be certified by the Department of Labor before the I-129 is filed, confirming the employer will pay the prevailing wage for the role and location.

Alternative routes include the **L-1 visa** (intracompany transferee — requires at least one year of employment with the sponsoring company abroad), the **O-1A** (extraordinary ability — high bar, no cap), and **TN** status for Canadian and Mexican nationals under the USMCA. Verify all current requirements at uscis.gov.

For a detailed look at finding software roles with H-1B sponsorship, see [Software Engineer Jobs in USA with Visa Sponsorship 2026](/software-engineer-jobs-in-usa-visa-sponsorship/).

### Canada — Global Talent Stream and Express Entry

Canada's **Global Talent Stream (GTS)**, a stream within the Temporary Foreign Worker Program, offers accelerated two-week processing for tech employers hiring in designated occupations (Category B, which includes software engineers, data scientists, and IT project managers among others). Employers must apply to Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) and receive a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) under the stream — or in some cases work with a GTS-designated referral partner.

The **Express Entry** system — which covers the Federal Skilled Worker Program, the Canadian Experience Class, and the Federal Skilled Trades Program — is points-based (using the Comprehensive Ranking System, CRS) and does not always require individual employer sponsorship, though a valid job offer from a Canadian employer with an LMIA can add significant CRS points. Verify current occupation lists and CRS cut-off scores at canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship.

### Germany — EU Blue Card and Opportunity Card

Germany's **EU Blue Card** is the primary sponsored route for non-EU tech professionals. Requirements include a recognised university degree and a job offer at or above the salary threshold for IT specialists (lower thresholds apply to shortage occupations — verify at bamf.de, as thresholds are updated). The application is made to the relevant German consulate or, for in-country changes, to the Ausländerbehörde.

Germany's **Chancenkarte** (Opportunity Card), introduced under the 2023 Skilled Immigration Act, allows qualified workers to enter Germany to look for work on a points basis, without a confirmed job offer. Tech professionals with a relevant degree and work experience may score sufficient points. This is a relatively new route and operational details continue to evolve — check bamf.de for current eligibility criteria.

### Australia — Employer-Sponsored and Skills Assessment Routes

Australia's main employer-sponsored route is the **Temporary Skill Shortage (TSS) visa (subclass 482)**, the Medium-Term stream of which covers most technology occupations. Employers must be an approved sponsor, nominate the position, and the candidate must have the skills and experience to fill it. Skills assessments — conducted by the relevant assessing authority (for ICT roles, typically the Australian Computer Society, ACS) — are required for some occupation categories.

The **Employer Nomination Scheme (ENS, subclass 186)** provides a permanent residence pathway for longer-term sponsored employees. Verify current occupation lists, salary requirements, and assessing authority requirements at immi.homeaffairs.gov.au.

## How to Find Tech Firms That Sponsor: A Practical Search Framework

Knowing that tech firms sponsor is one thing; finding the specific ones with open roles in your discipline is another. The following framework is the most effective approach for 2026.

### Step 1: Use the Official Sponsor Register

In the UK, the Home Office publishes a regularly updated list of all licensed sponsors, downloadable as a CSV file from gov.uk. Filter by "Skilled Worker" licence type and cross-reference with technology company names or locations. This tells you with certainty which firms can legally sponsor — removing all guesswork.

### Step 2: Target Job Boards That Filter for Sponsorship

General job boards surface sponsored roles inconsistently. Boards that allow explicit "visa sponsorship" filtering, or sector-specific boards used heavily by tech recruiters, are more efficient. Treat any listing claiming sponsorship as unconfirmed until the employer confirms they hold a licence and are prepared to assign a COS for the specific role.

### Step 3: Use LinkedIn Signals Strategically

Search for roles using the "on-site/remote" and location filters alongside keywords such as "visa sponsorship available" or "Tier 2 sponsorship considered." Cross-reference the employer with the official register before investing significant application effort.

### Step 4: Target Firms With a Track Record

USCIS publishes annual H-1B employer data. The UK Home Office publishes transparency data on COS usage. These are public records that show which firms actually sponsor at volume — not just which ones say they do in a job posting. Reviewing them is worth the time if you are making long-term career decisions around sponsorship.

### Step 5: Network With Sponsored Workers Already in the Industry

Online communities — particularly those organised around specific diaspora communities or professional groups — are an underused source of intelligence on which firms sponsor well, which are bureaucratically slow, and which retract offers when the sponsorship reality becomes clear. This kind of ground-level information complements official data.

## Common Mistakes Tech Job Seekers Make — and How to Fix Them

1. **Applying to firms not on the sponsor register.**
   Fix: Check the UK Home Office register (or the USCIS H-1B data for the US) before applying. Time spent on an application to an unlicensed employer is wasted.

2. **Assuming "we welcome international applicants" means sponsorship.**
   Fix: Ask directly and early. Use a clear, professional question: "Is this role available to candidates who require a Skilled Worker visa, and does your firm hold a current sponsor licence?" See [How to Ask an Employer About Visa Sponsorship (2026)](/how-to-ask-employer-about-visa-sponsorship/).

3. **Underestimating the salary threshold.**
   Fix: Check the current going rate for your specific occupation code, not just the headline general threshold. In some high-demand tech roles, the going rate exceeds the general threshold significantly. Check the occupation code appendix in the Home Office Skilled Worker guidance.

4. **Relying solely on the H-1B lottery.**
   Fix: Explore cap-exempt employers (universities, research institutes), L-1 pathways if you work for a multinational, O-1A if your credentials are genuinely exceptional, and non-US markets running parallel searches.

5. **Failing to factor in the full timeline.**
   Fix: If an employer needs to apply for a sponsor licence before sponsoring you, add several months to the expected start date. Factor this into any notice period or competing offer negotiations.

6. **Paying anyone for a sponsorship letter or job placement.**
   Fix: Do not pay. No legitimate employer or recruiter charges a worker for sponsorship. This is a hard rule with no exceptions. Report any such request to the relevant authority.

7. **Ignoring the compliance obligations that come with sponsored status.**
   Fix: As a sponsored worker, you have obligations too — including not taking on work outside your COS, reporting changes to your employer and (via your employer) to the Home Office, and maintaining your immigration conditions. Understand these before you sign.

## A Note on Employer Obligations — and Why They Matter to You

When a tech firm sponsors you, they are taking on legal responsibilities that run for the duration of your visa. They must conduct right to work checks, maintain accurate records, report specified changes to the Home Office (including if your role changes materially or if you stop working for them), and cooperate with any Home Office audit. If they fail in these duties, they risk losing their sponsor licence — which would directly affect your immigration status.

This is why the quality of an employer's HR infrastructure matters as much as the job offer itself. A firm that has recently obtained a sponsor licence for the first time, or that has limited HR capacity, may find compliance more challenging. It is reasonable to ask during the recruitment process who manages their sponsor licence and what their experience of sponsoring overseas hires has been. You can also check whether any compliance action has been taken against a sponsor (suspensions and revocations are published by the Home Office).

For more on employer obligations, see [Hiring International Workers: Employer Obligations UK 2026](/hiring-international-workers-employer-obligations/).

## The Visa Sponsorship Process: What to Expect End to End

From the moment you identify a tech firm that sponsors to the moment you are legally working in your new country, there are typically five stages:

1. **Application and offer.** You apply, interview, and receive a conditional offer. The offer should state that it is contingent on successful visa sponsorship.
2. **Employer licence verification or application.** The employer confirms they hold a valid sponsor licence (or, in some cases, applies for one).
3. **Certificate of Sponsorship / petition filing.** The employer assigns a COS (UK) or files a petition such as Form I-129 (US) on your behalf. You cannot begin this step yourself — it is always employer-initiated.
4. **Visa application.** You apply for the visa using the COS reference or petition approval notice, pay applicable fees (including the Immigration Health Surcharge in the UK), and attend a biometrics appointment if required.
5. **Arrival and right to work confirmation.** You provide evidence of your right to work to the employer (a legal requirement — not optional). In the UK this is a digital share code check via the Home Office online service.

For a step-by-step walkthrough of the full process from a candidate's perspective, see [Visa Sponsorship Application Process End to End (2026)](/visa-sponsorship-application-process-end-to-end/).

## Where to Get Help

This guide gives you the framework, but specific eligibility decisions — whether your degree is recognised, whether your occupation code qualifies, whether a particular employer's sponsor licence is in good standing — should be verified with official sources and, where the stakes are high, with a qualified immigration professional.

In the UK, look for a solicitor regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA) with an immigration specialism, or an adviser registered with the Office of the Immigration Services Commissioner (OISC) at Level 2 or 3. In the US, use an attorney admitted to a US state bar with an immigration practice. Avoid anyone who is not regulated and charges on the basis of a promised outcome.

The official sources you should bookmark:
- UK: gov.uk/skilled-worker-visa and the Home Office Register of Licensed Sponsors
- US: uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/h-1b-specialty-occupations
- Canada: canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/services/foreign-workers/global-talent.html
- Germany: bamf.de
- Australia: immi.homeaffairs.gov.au

That combination of rigour and realism is what separates candidates who land sponsored roles from those who spend years in frustration.
