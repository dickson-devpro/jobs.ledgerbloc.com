**US citizens living abroad are legally required to file a federal tax return with the IRS every year if their income exceeds the standard filing threshold — regardless of where they live, where they earn, or how long they have been away. Failing to file does not make the obligation disappear; it creates penalties that can compound quietly for years. Understanding the forms, elections and deadlines involved is the only way to stay on the right side of the IRS while building a life overseas.**

---

## Who This Guide Is For

This article is written for US citizens (and, where noted, long-term permanent residents and certain dual-status filers) who are living or working outside the United States and need to understand their federal tax obligations. It covers the key concepts, forms and processes involved in 2026 filing. It is general information only — not tax advice for your specific situation. Where your circumstances are complex, the guide signposts you to appropriate professional resources.

---

## Why the US Taxes Citizens Abroad: Citizenship-Based Taxation Explained

Most countries use residence-based taxation: you pay local tax where you live. The United States, alongside very few other countries, uses citizenship-based taxation. The legal foundation is long-standing, and the IRS enforces it through a web of international information-sharing agreements under the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA), which requires foreign financial institutions to report US-connected account holders to the US government.

The practical consequence is straightforward: if you hold a US passport — or a green card you have not formally relinquished — the IRS considers you a taxpayer. Moving to Berlin, Sydney, or São Paulo does not change that. What changes is which forms you file and which reliefs you may be entitled to.

## Key Filing Deadlines for Americans Abroad in 2026

Getting deadlines wrong is one of the most common and avoidable mistakes expat filers make.

### Standard and Automatic Extended Deadline

The standard federal income tax filing deadline is **15 April**. Americans living and working outside the United States on that date receive an **automatic two-month extension** to **16 June 2026** (when the 15th falls on a weekend or public holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day — confirm the exact date at IRS.gov each year). This extension applies to filing, not to payment: any tax owed is still technically due by 15 April, and interest accrues on unpaid balances from that date.

### Extension to October

You can request a further extension to **15 October 2026** by filing **Form 4868** (Application for Automatic Extension of Time To File) by the June deadline. This is an extension of time to file, not to pay. If you believe you will owe tax, estimating and paying by the earlier deadline limits interest exposure.

### FBAR Deadline

The **FinCEN Form 114** (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts, commonly called the FBAR) has a deadline of **15 April**, with an automatic extension to **15 October**. This is filed separately from your tax return through the FinCEN BSA E-Filing System at bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov — not through the IRS. Confirm the current deadline at fincen.gov.

## The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555)

### What It Does

The FEIE is one of the most powerful tools available to US citizens abroad. It allows you to exclude a set amount of **foreign earned income** — wages, salaries, self-employment income earned for services performed in a foreign country — from US federal income tax. The exclusion amount is adjusted for inflation each year; for the 2025 tax year it was in the region of $126,500 (illustrative figure — verify the exact current-year amount at IRS.gov before filing, as it changes annually).

Crucially, the exclusion applies only to **earned income**. Investment income, rental income, pensions, and certain other passive income streams are not excluded by Form 2555 and remain fully taxable.

### Qualifying Tests

To claim the FEIE you must meet one of two tests:

**Bona Fide Residence Test:** You have been a genuine resident of a foreign country for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year. "Resident" here is determined by facts and circumstances — local housing, family ties, social connections, intent — not simply physical presence. Having a tourist visa or temporary work permit in some countries can complicate this.

**Physical Presence Test:** You were physically present in a foreign country or countries for at least **330 full days** during any consecutive 12-month period. This test is mechanical and more straightforward to document.

### The Foreign Housing Exclusion or Deduction

Form 2555 also covers the **Foreign Housing Exclusion** (for employees) or **Foreign Housing Deduction** (for self-employed individuals). This allows an additional amount above the base FEIE to cover qualifying housing expenses such as rent, utilities, and certain insurance costs. The allowable amounts vary by geographic location and are updated annually. See IRS Publication 54 for the current table of location-specific limits.

## The Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116)

### When It Is Used Instead of (or Alongside) the FEIE

If you live in a high-tax country where local income tax rates exceed US rates, the **Foreign Tax Credit** may be more advantageous than the FEIE, or may eliminate US liability on income not covered by the exclusion. Form 1116 provides a dollar-for-dollar credit against your US tax liability for creditable foreign taxes paid or accrued on foreign-source income.

You cannot claim the FEIE and the FTC on the **same income**, but you can use the FEIE to exclude part of your income and the FTC on the remaining foreign-source income that falls outside the exclusion — subject to detailed rules around the foreign tax credit limitation baskets. This interaction is an area where professional advice pays for itself.

### Passive Income Basket vs General Category

The FTC operates through different "baskets" of income — the two most common for expat employees are the general category basket (covering wages and active income) and the passive category basket (covering dividends, interest, and royalties). Excess credits in one basket generally cannot offset liability in another. If you have multiple income types, the limitation calculation can become complex quickly.

## FBAR and FATCA: Foreign Account Reporting

These two obligations are frequently confused, and non-compliance penalties for both can be severe.

### FBAR (FinCEN Form 114)

If the **aggregate highest balance** across all your foreign financial accounts exceeded **$10,000** at any point during the calendar year — even for a single day — you must file the FBAR. "Financial accounts" includes bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and certain insurance and pension products. Filing is electronic, through the BSA E-Filing System, and is entirely separate from your IRS tax return.

Penalties for wilful non-filing have historically been substantial (in some cases greater than the account balance itself). Even non-wilful violations carry civil penalties. Verify current penalty structures at fincen.gov or with a qualified adviser.

### Form 8938 (FATCA)

Form 8938 is filed with your Form 1040. The filing thresholds are higher than the FBAR and depend on your filing status and whether you are living abroad:

- Single or married filing separately (living abroad): generally $200,000 on the last day of the year, or $300,000 at any point during the year (illustrative; verify at IRS.gov)
- Married filing jointly (living abroad): generally $400,000 / $600,000 respectively (illustrative; verify at IRS.gov)

Even if you do not meet the threshold for Form 8938, you may still owe an FBAR. The two requirements are independent.

## Self-Employment Abroad: Special Considerations

Americans working as freelancers, contractors, or business owners abroad face an additional layer: **self-employment tax**. Even if you exclude all your earned income under the FEIE, the exclusion does not reduce your net self-employment earnings for the purpose of calculating Social Security and Medicare tax (SE tax). You owe SE tax on net earnings above a modest threshold regardless of the income exclusion.

The US has **Totalization Agreements** with a number of countries — including the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Australia and others — designed to prevent double Social Security taxation. Whether a Totalization Agreement exempts you from US SE tax, or the host country's equivalent, depends on which country you are posted to, by whom, and for how long. Check the current list at SSA.gov and confirm your specific situation with a tax professional.

## State Tax Obligations: The Often-Overlooked Layer

Filing federally is not the end of the picture. Some US states assert the right to tax former residents who retain connections — a driving licence, a registered vehicle, voter registration, bank accounts, property — to that state. States with historically aggressive positions on this include **California, New York, and Virginia**, though rules change and each case turns on specific facts.

If you left the US without formally establishing domicile elsewhere in the US before moving abroad, or if you return to the same state regularly, consult a tax professional about your state filing obligations. Some expats are genuinely surprised by state tax bills years after leaving.

## Illustrative Worked Examples

The following examples are illustrative only, using approximate round figures for clarity. They are not tax advice, and actual outcomes depend on individual facts, elections, applicable treaties, and current law.

### Example 1: Employee in the UK, Below High-Tax Crossover

**Situation:** Mia is a US citizen working as a marketing manager in London. In the 2025 tax year she earned the equivalent of approximately $95,000 in sterling wages from a UK employer. She pays UK income tax and National Insurance. She has been resident in the UK for two full years.

**Possible approach:** Mia may qualify for the FEIE under the Bona Fide Residence Test and could potentially exclude her entire earned income (which is below the approximate $126,500 exclusion ceiling). If she does so, her US federal income tax liability on that earned income could be reduced to nil. She would still need to file Form 1040, attach Form 2555, and check whether any residual non-excluded income (such as US-source investment income) creates US tax liability. She must also assess FBAR obligations if her UK bank accounts held more than $10,000 in aggregate at any point during the year.

### Example 2: Self-Employed Contractor in Germany, High Earner

**Situation:** James is a US citizen working as an independent IT consultant in Germany. He earned the equivalent of approximately $180,000 in the 2025 tax year from German clients. He has been physically present in Germany for over 330 days in a 12-month period.

**Possible approach:** James qualifies under the Physical Presence Test. He can exclude approximately $126,500 (illustrative) under the FEIE on Form 2555, but the remaining ~$53,500 is still exposed to US federal income tax. Germany's income tax rates are relatively high, so he may be able to use the Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) on the non-excluded portion to reduce or eliminate his US liability on that income. However, he will owe US self-employment tax on his net self-employment earnings regardless of the FEIE. He should also check whether the US-Germany Totalization Agreement affects his Social Security obligations. FBAR and potentially Form 8938 will apply given his German business and bank accounts.

## Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures: For Late Filers

If you have not filed US returns for several years because you did not know you had to, the **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures** provide a route to compliance. There are two versions:

**Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures (SFOP):** For US citizens residing abroad who have not wilfully evaded tax. Requires filing three years of delinquent income tax returns, six years of FBARs, paying any outstanding tax and interest, and certifying non-wilfulness. Currently, no accuracy or delinquency penalties apply to eligible filers.

**Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures (SDOP):** For those who have been physically in the US during the relevant period. Carries a 5% offshore penalty.

The critical issue is the **non-wilfulness certification**. Submitting a streamlined application when your conduct was actually wilful can increase your exposure significantly. This is not a DIY situation — engage a qualified US tax attorney or CPA with expat compliance experience before making the certification.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

1. **Missing the FBAR deadline or confusing it with the tax return deadline.**
   *Fix:* Diarise both deadlines separately. The FBAR goes to FinCEN (bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov), not the IRS, and covers any year in which your accounts collectively exceeded $10,000.

2. **Assuming the FEIE eliminates all US tax.**
   *Fix:* The FEIE covers foreign earned income only. Dividends, interest, capital gains, rental income, and US-source income remain taxable. Run a full income reconciliation before assuming your liability is zero.

3. **Choosing the FEIE without considering the FTC.**
   *Fix:* If you live in a high-tax country, the FTC may produce a better outcome and preserves access to certain retirement account contribution benefits. Compare both scenarios — ideally with a tax professional — before making an election.

4. **Failing to file Form 8938 alongside the FBAR.**
   *Fix:* If your foreign financial assets exceed the applicable FATCA thresholds, both obligations apply independently. Non-filing of Form 8938 can result in penalties and can suspend the statute of limitations on your entire return.

5. **Ignoring state tax obligations after moving abroad.**
   *Fix:* Review any remaining ties to your former US state of residence. If you have not properly severed domicile, some states will continue to assert a tax claim. Document the steps you took to establish foreign or out-of-state domicile.

6. **Using a domestic US accountant unfamiliar with expat-specific rules.**
   *Fix:* Form 2555, Form 1116, FBAR, Form 8938, Totalization Agreements, and the interaction between the FEIE and self-employment tax are specialist areas. A general-practice accountant may miss elections that save significant money or fail to file required information returns. Seek a CPA or enrolled agent with a demonstrable expat tax practice.

7. **Overlooking foreign pension and retirement account reporting.**
   *Fix:* Foreign pension schemes, employer-provided retirement funds, and certain life insurance products can trigger reporting obligations including Form 3520, Form 3520-A, or Form 8621 (for passive foreign investment companies). The rules are highly fact-specific; get professional advice if you hold or contribute to non-US retirement arrangements.

## How to File: Practical Steps for 2026

1. **Gather your income documentation.** Collect all foreign payslips, employer statements, freelance income records, bank interest statements, dividend vouchers, and any other income source. Convert all amounts to US dollars using the appropriate exchange rate — the IRS accepts the annual average rate published by the US Treasury or by the IRS itself; see IRS.gov for the current guidance.

2. **Determine your qualifying test.** Assess whether you meet the Bona Fide Residence Test or Physical Presence Test for the FEIE. Keep records of all travel days, as the Physical Presence Test requires precise day counts.

3. **Identify all reportable accounts.** List every foreign financial account — chequing, savings, brokerage, pension, insurance — and determine aggregate highest balance for FBAR purposes and total maximum value for Form 8938 purposes.

4. **Choose FEIE, FTC, or combination.** For most employees in moderate-tax countries, the FEIE is the starting point. For high earners in high-tax jurisdictions, the FTC may be more beneficial. See [US Income Tax Brackets and Rates Explained (2026)](/us-income-tax-brackets-rates-explained/) for context on how the US rate structure interacts with these elections.

5. **Prepare and file Form 1040 with all required attachments.** Expats can file electronically using IRS Free File (if income-eligible) or paid tax software. Paper returns from abroad can be mailed to the address specified in the Form 1040 instructions for overseas filers.

6. **File the FBAR separately** at bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov by the applicable deadline.

7. **Retain records.** Keep copies of all filed returns, FBAR confirmations, and supporting documents for at least six years — longer if you have any complex positions or are relying on a treaty.

For a detailed walkthrough of the federal return itself, see [How to File a US Tax Return Step by Step (2026)](/how-to-file-us-tax-return-step-by-step/).

## Renouncing US Citizenship: Tax Implications

Some long-term expats eventually consider renouncing US citizenship to escape citizenship-based taxation permanently. This is a serious, irreversible step with significant tax consequences. **Covered expatriates** — those who meet income, net worth, or compliance thresholds — may be subject to the **expatriation tax** under IRC Section 877A, which treats most assets as if sold on the day before expatriation and taxes the resulting deemed gain.

Renunciation also requires being up to date with five years of tax returns and FBARs. This is genuinely one area where the advice of a qualified US tax attorney — not simply a CPA — is warranted before taking any steps. If you are also navigating immigration implications in your host country (for example, applying for citizenship abroad while holding a work visa), refer to resources specific to that jurisdiction.

## A Note on Tax Treaties

The US has income tax treaties with many countries, including the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Australia, Canada, Japan, and others. Treaties can modify the default rules — for example, by clarifying which country has taxing rights over certain income types such as government pensions, social security benefits, or income from self-employment. Relying on a treaty position typically requires filing **Form 8833** (Treaty-Based Return Position Disclosure) with your return.

Treaties are complex documents, and the interaction between treaty provisions and domestic US law (including the "saving clause", which generally preserves the US right to tax its own citizens) is a specialist area. Do not assume a treaty eliminates your US filing obligation; it rarely does.

## Getting Professional Help

Given the complexity of this area, professional help is money well spent for many expat filers:

- A **Certified Public Accountant (CPA)** with expat specialisation can prepare your returns, make optimal elections, and advise on FBAR and FATCA compliance.
- An **Enrolled Agent (EA)** authorised to practise before the IRS is another qualified option.
- A **US tax attorney** is appropriate for complex situations: back-filing, streamlined procedures, expatriation planning, foreign corporation reporting, and disputes with the IRS.

The IRS maintains a directory of credentialed tax professionals at irs.gov/tax-professionals. The American Citizens Abroad (ACA) and Democrats Abroad Tax Committee both maintain resources for expats. If your situation involves navigating immigration alongside tax — for example, you are working abroad on a sponsored visa and considering a move to a different country — understanding the financial picture is part of making an informed decision. ## Summary: Your Expat Tax Compliance Checklist for 2026

- Confirm whether your income exceeds the filing threshold (IRS.gov)
- Assess eligibility for FEIE (Form 2555) — Bona Fide Residence or Physical Presence Test
- Consider whether the Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) is more beneficial, in whole or in part
- Check all foreign accounts against the $10,000 FBAR threshold — file FinCEN Form 114 if triggered
- Assess Form 8938 obligations under FATCA
- Check for state tax obligations in your former state of residence
- Determine whether any foreign pensions, retirement accounts, or corporate interests require additional information returns
- File Form 1040 with all attachments by the applicable deadline (16 June 2026 automatic extension for qualifying expats; 15 October with Form 4868)
- Retain all records for a minimum of six years

All thresholds, deadlines, and form requirements should be verified against current IRS guidance at IRS.gov and FinCEN guidance at fincen.gov, as they are subject to change. For guidance specific to your circumstances, consult a qualified CPA, enrolled agent, or US tax attorney with experience in expatriate taxation.
