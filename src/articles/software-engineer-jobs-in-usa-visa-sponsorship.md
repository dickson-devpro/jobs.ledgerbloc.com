## Software Engineering Visa Sponsorship in the USA: What You Actually Need to Know

Software engineering is among the most consistently sponsored occupations in the United States. Employers in technology, finance, healthcare, defence and almost every other sector compete for engineers, and the domestic supply of qualified candidates has not kept pace with demand for decades. If you have a relevant degree and demonstrable skills, you are entering a market where sponsorship is a normal part of hiring — not a favour an employer is doing you.

The primary visa route for most international candidates is the **H-1B specialty occupation visa**, an employer-led, capped, lottery-based programme administered by US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). A minority of candidates enter through cap-exempt routes or pursue employer-sponsored permanent residence from the outset. This guide covers what the work involves, what it pays, how sponsorship actually works, and what gets candidates rejected.

---

## What the Work Actually Involves

Software engineering in the United States covers a wide range of day-to-day realities depending on sector, company size and specialism. At a large technology company in Seattle or the San Francisco Bay Area, you might spend your time on distributed systems, API design, machine learning infrastructure or developer tooling, working in cross-functional product teams with sprint cycles, code review culture and significant autonomy over technical decisions. At a mid-size fintech in New York, the work is more likely to involve regulatory constraints, low-latency systems and legacy modernisation. At a defence contractor in Virginia, security clearance considerations shape everything, and the pace is different again.

What most US software engineering roles share: version control (Git), continuous integration and deployment pipelines, collaborative code review, and an expectation that you can write and reason about distributed systems at some level of abstraction. Communication skills matter considerably — engineers in the US are expected to contribute to architectural discussions, write documentation, and work directly with product managers and sometimes customers.

### Why Employers Struggle to Fill These Roles

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects strong growth in software developer occupations through the late 2020s, and the number of computer science graduates produced domestically has not filled the gap. Competition from well-funded startups and large technology companies drives up salary expectations for US citizens and permanent residents, making it economically rational for employers to invest in the cost and administrative burden of H-1B sponsorship for candidates who offer the right skills. Particular shortages exist in areas such as embedded systems, cybersecurity engineering, machine learning infrastructure, cloud-native architecture and certain legacy languages (COBOL, for example, in financial services). If your specialism sits in one of these areas, you are more attractive to sponsors.

## What Software Engineers Earn in the USA

Salaries for sponsored software engineers vary enormously by location, level, employer type and specialism. The figures below are indicative ranges to give you a sense of the market — they are not guarantees, and actual offers depend on individual negotiation, the employer's pay bands and the prevailing wage requirements that apply to your specific role and location.

| Level | Indicative range (USD/year) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Junior / Entry-level | $85,000 – $120,000 | Less common for sponsored candidates given H-1B costs |
| Mid-level (3–6 yrs exp) | $120,000 – $170,000 | Most common H-1B sponsorship tier |
| Senior (7+ yrs exp) | $160,000 – $220,000 | High sponsor demand; faster petition justification |
| Staff / Principal | $200,000 – $300,000+ | Often includes significant equity component |
| FAANG / top-tier tech | Total comp can reach $400,000+ | Base salary is only part of the picture |

Salaries are almost always quoted as annual base salary in USD. At larger technology companies, total compensation typically includes restricted stock units (RSUs), a performance bonus and benefits. When evaluating an offer, ask for the total compensation breakdown — the base alone can be misleading.

**What moves salary up:** working in the San Francisco Bay Area, New York City or Seattle; specialising in machine learning, security engineering or cloud infrastructure; having experience at a well-known technology company; seniority level.

**What keeps it lower:** working for a smaller employer outside a major tech hub; roles classified at a lower Department of Labor wage level; generalist rather than specialist skills.

The employer is legally required to pay at least the **prevailing wage** for the role and location, as determined under the Labor Condition Application (LCA) process. This is a legal floor, not a ceiling.

## The H-1B Visa Route in Detail

### Who Initiates the Process

The H-1B is an employer-led visa. You cannot apply for it yourself, and no agent or intermediary can obtain one for you independently of an employer. The employer — referred to as the petitioner — drives every step. If someone is offering to get you an H-1B without a named, verifiable US employer behind it, that is a scam.

### Step 1 — The Labor Condition Application

Before filing anything with USCIS, the employer must obtain a certified Labor Condition Application (LCA) from the Department of Labor (DOL). The LCA attests that the employer will pay the prevailing wage, that employing you will not adversely affect similarly employed US workers, and that there is no relevant strike or lockout. The DOL publishes prevailing wage data by occupation and location. Verify the rules at **dol.gov** and **uscis.gov** — thresholds and procedures change.

### Step 2 — Electronic Registration and the Lottery

USCIS opens an electronic registration window, typically in early March, for the following fiscal year's cap. Your employer (or their immigration attorney) submits a registration on your behalf. There is a statutory annual cap of 65,000 H-1B visas under the general category, plus an additional 20,000 reserved for beneficiaries who hold a US master's degree or higher. If registrations exceed the cap — which they have done consistently in recent years — USCIS conducts a random lottery to determine which registrations advance to a full petition.

Being selected in the lottery means your employer may now file a complete H-1B petition. It does not mean your visa is approved. Not being selected means you cannot proceed under the cap that year unless you qualify for a cap-exempt route.

### Step 3 — The Full Petition

If selected, the employer files Form I-129 (Petition for Nonimmigrant Worker) along with the certified LCA, evidence that the role qualifies as a specialty occupation, evidence of your qualifications, and the applicable filing fees. USCIS adjudicates the petition on either standard processing (timeline varies; check uscis.gov for current processing times) or premium processing, which as of the time of writing targets 15 business days for adjudication. Premium processing incurs an additional government fee that the employer must pay.

### Step 4 — Consular Processing

If you are outside the US, an approved petition is followed by an application for an H-1B visa stamp at a US consulate or embassy. This involves Form DS-160, a visa interview, biometrics and review of your supporting documents. Consular appointments can be backlogged — check wait times at travel.state.gov and factor this into your timeline.

### What You Must Provide

Your employer's immigration attorney will typically request: academic transcripts and degree certificates, evidence of relevant work experience (employment letters, pay stubs, tax records from previous employers), your passport, and any prior US immigration history. If your degree is from outside the US, the employer may require a credential evaluation from a NACES-member organisation to establish degree equivalency.

### Cap-Exempt Alternatives

If you are offered a role at a qualifying institution of higher education, a non-profit affiliated with such an institution, or a non-profit or government research organisation, the position may be cap-exempt. This means the employer can file an H-1B petition at any time of year without going through the lottery. These roles are relatively limited but worth knowing about, particularly for candidates targeting research or academic positions.

## Qualifications and Licensing Requirements

Unlike nursing or medicine, software engineering in the United States does not require a government-issued professional licence to practise in most roles. However, the H-1B specialty occupation standard imposes its own qualification threshold: USCIS generally requires that the position normally requires at least a US bachelor's degree or its equivalent in a **directly related** field. A bachelor's degree in computer science, software engineering, information technology, mathematics or a closely related technical discipline is the most defensible credential.

Foreign degrees are accepted but must be evaluated for US equivalency. If your degree title or grading system differs substantially from US conventions, invest in a credential evaluation report early — it is often the first document an employer's immigration attorney needs.

Work experience may substitute for a degree in some cases, but USCIS applies a three-for-one rule (three years of specialised experience equates to one year of formal education) and scrutinises these cases more heavily. If you lack a relevant bachelor's degree, discuss this honestly with a prospective employer before they commit to sponsorship — a petition built on a weak educational equivalency argument is a common source of denials and Requests for Evidence.

Candidates targeting roles that involve government contracts or access to classified information should be aware that non-US citizens face significant restrictions on obtaining security clearances. This is not an H-1B issue per se, but it effectively closes off a meaningful segment of the US software engineering market to sponsored candidates.

## Which Employers Actually Sponsor Software Engineers

Not every US company that posts software engineering jobs will sponsor H-1B workers. The cost and administrative burden mean that smaller employers without established immigration programmes rarely do. The employers most likely to sponsor are:

**Large technology companies** — The major Silicon Valley and Seattle-based technology companies are among the largest H-1B sponsors in the US every year. They have in-house immigration teams and established processes.

**Financial services and fintech firms** — Banks, hedge funds, payment processors and fintech companies sponsor at scale, particularly for engineering roles involving data infrastructure, trading systems and regulatory technology.

**Consulting and staffing firms** — A number of technology consulting companies use H-1B sponsorship as a business model, placing engineers at client sites. This is legal but creates a different employment dynamic — understand who your day-to-day employer actually is.

**Cloud and enterprise software vendors** — Companies building and maintaining large-scale B2B software products consistently sponsor internationally.

**Healthcare technology and life sciences companies** — As health systems digitise, demand for engineers with domain experience in healthcare data, imaging, and clinical systems has grown.

**How to identify genuine sponsors:** Search USCIS's publicly available H-1B employer data (released annually) to verify that a company has a recent history of approved H-1B petitions. Use the DOL's LCA disclosure data. If an employer claims to sponsor but cannot name their immigration attorney or explain their previous sponsorship history, treat that with scepticism.

## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected

1. **Mismatched degree and job title.** If your degree is in civil engineering and the role is described as a software engineer, USCIS may issue a Request for Evidence or deny the petition on specialty occupation grounds. Fix: ensure the employer's job description references duties that align with your actual degree field. Some employers adjust role descriptions in consultation with immigration counsel — this is legitimate when the duties genuinely match.

2. **Inadequate specialty occupation evidence.** USCIS looks for evidence that a bachelor's degree in a specific field is a normal requirement for the role, not just a general preference. Fix: the employer should provide industry surveys, internal job requirement documentation and expert opinion letters if needed.

3. **Poorly documented foreign degree equivalency.** An evaluation that simply states "equivalent to a US bachelor's degree" without specifying the field may not satisfy USCIS. Fix: use a NACES-member evaluator and ensure the report specifies the equivalent discipline.

4. **Employer claiming cap-exempt status incorrectly.** Some employers misrepresent their eligibility for cap exemption. Fix: if an employer tells you the role is cap-exempt, ask them to explain specifically which exemption applies and confirm it with the immigration attorney.

5. **Relying on a petition filed by a consulting firm for a role at a third-party client site.** USCIS scrutinises employer-employee relationships in consulting arrangements heavily. Fix: ensure there is a clear, documentable employer-employee relationship and that the end-client's role requirements are part of the petition.

6. **Missing or thin work experience documentation.** Experience letters from previous employers that lack specific dates, job titles and duties raise questions during adjudication. Fix: gather detailed employment verification letters before the petition is filed.

7. **Applying to employers who have never sponsored before without verifying intent.** Some employers list "visa sponsorship available" speculatively. Fix: ask directly and early whether the company has previously filed H-1B petitions and whether they have immigration counsel engaged.

8. **Paying anyone for the job or the visa.** Legitimate employers do not charge candidates. If you are asked to pay a recruiter, an agent or an employer for job placement or visa filing, walk away. This is both a scam indicator and potentially illegal.

## Realistic Timeline: Registration to Arrival

| Stage | When it happens | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Job offer and LCA filing | Employer files LCA ~60 days before registration | 1–3 weeks for DOL certification |
| H-1B electronic registration | Typically early March | 2-week window |
| Lottery result | USCIS notifies, typically late March | — |
| Full petition filing | April 1 or after selection | 1–4 weeks to prepare and file |
| USCIS adjudication (premium processing) | After filing | ~15 business days |
| USCIS adjudication (standard processing) | After filing | Varies; check uscis.gov for current times |
| Consular visa interview appointment | After petition approval | Weeks to months depending on post |
| H-1B validity start date | October 1 (cap-subject) | — |
| **Total: registration to US start date** | — | **6–10 months for cap-subject cases** |

Cap-exempt cases do not follow this calendar and can proceed year-round, with timelines driven by USCIS processing only. Premium processing is available for cap-exempt petitions and can reduce adjudication time significantly.

## Scam Warning: Protecting Yourself

The demand for US software engineering sponsorship creates a target-rich environment for fraud. Understand these rules clearly:

**No legitimate employer charges you for a job.** If a company or recruiter asks you to pay a fee to be considered for a role, to cover "processing costs," or to secure your place in a visa programme, it is a scam.

**No legitimate agent can obtain an H-1B for you without a real employer.** Agents who claim to have H-1B "slots" available, or who promise to get you a visa for a fee without naming a specific employer with a real job opening, are lying. H-1B visas are tied to a specific employer and a specific role.

**Verify the employer independently.** Before sharing personal documents, search the company on USCIS's H-1B employer data portal and the DOL LCA disclosure database. Confirm the company's physical address, registered status and that the person contacting you works for the company they claim to represent. A LinkedIn profile and a website alone are not sufficient verification.

**Use only official government websites.** The relevant sources for US immigration rules are **uscis.gov** (USCIS) and **travel.state.gov** (Department of State, for visa stamping). Information from third-party websites, including this guide, may become outdated — always verify current thresholds, fees and procedures at the official sources before making decisions.

**If something feels off, it probably is.** Legitimate employers do not pressure candidates to commit immediately, do not request money, and do not promise guaranteed visa approval. No one can guarantee H-1B selection in the lottery or visa approval — anyone who does is misrepresenting the process.
