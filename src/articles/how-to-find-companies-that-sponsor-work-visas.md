**Companies that sponsor work visas are not hidden — they are publicly listed on government registers in the UK, USA, Australia and most other major destination countries. The real skill is knowing which register to use, how to cross-reference it with live job postings, and how to approach employers in a way that moves your application forward. This guide covers all of that, for every major destination, in plain terms.**

---

## Who This Guide Is For

This article is written for **job seekers** researching how to find employers willing and legally able to sponsor an overseas worker for a work visa. It covers the UK Skilled Worker route in most depth, because the UK's publicly searchable Register of Licensed Sponsors makes it one of the most transparent systems in the world, but it also addresses the USA, Australia, Canada and continental Europe.

Before diving into tactics, it helps to be clear on what sponsorship actually involves. If you want a grounding in the mechanics first, read [What Visa Sponsorship Actually Means for a Job Seeker](/what-visa-sponsorship-means-for-job-seekers/) before continuing.

## Start With the Official Government Registers

### The UK Register of Licensed Sponsors

The single most powerful tool for UK job seekers is the **Register of Licensed Sponsors**, published and maintained by the Home Office. It is free, publicly accessible, and downloadable as a spreadsheet from GOV.UK. As of 2026, it contains well over 100,000 organisations holding a Skilled Worker licence — verify the current figure and download the latest version at [www.gov.uk/government/publications/register-of-licensed-sponsors-workers](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/register-of-licensed-sponsors-workers).

The register records each organisation's name, town or city, and the type of licence held (Skilled Worker, Global Business Mobility, and so on). It does not list which specific roles they are currently hiring for — that step requires cross-referencing with job boards. But it immediately answers the question: does this employer have the legal authority to sponsor me?

**How to use the register effectively:**

1. Download the spreadsheet.
2. Filter by your target sector or city using the organisation name and sub-type columns.
3. Build a target list of employers in your field who hold an active licence.
4. Search those employers on LinkedIn, Indeed, and their own careers pages for live vacancies.

This reverse approach — starting with verified sponsors, then finding their vacancies — is more reliable than starting with job boards and hoping the employer can sponsor.

You can also check whether a specific company holds a licence using [How to Check If a Company Has a UK Sponsor Licence](/how-to-check-if-company-holds-sponsor-licence/), which walks through the search process in detail.

### The US H-1B Employer Data Hub

For the United States, USCIS publishes the **H-1B Employer Data Hub**, searchable by employer name and fiscal year. It shows the number of initial approvals, continuing approvals, and denials for each employer. A company with hundreds of approvals over several years is demonstrably experienced in sponsoring H-1B workers — a meaningful signal when you are deciding where to focus your energy.

The hub is available at [www.uscis.gov/tools/reports-and-studies/h-1b-employer-data-hub](https://www.uscis.gov/tools/reports-and-studies/h-1b-employer-data-hub). Note that the H-1B is subject to an annual cap and a lottery, so even a well-resourced employer cannot guarantee selection. For roles in cap-exempt organisations — certain universities, non-profits affiliated with higher education, and government research entities — the lottery does not apply, which makes them strategically attractive. For individual eligibility questions, consult a US immigration attorney.

Beyond H-1B, employers sponsoring workers for EB-2 or EB-3 permanent residence (green card) petitions file **PERM Labour Certification** applications through the Department of Labor. The DOL's Foreign Labor Certification Data Center publishes disclosure data that names employers and occupations — a useful secondary research tool.

### Australia: The Approved Sponsor Register

Australia's Temporary Skill Shortage (TSS) visa (subclass 482) and the Employer Nomination Scheme (subclass 186) both require the employer to be an **approved sponsor** with the Department of Home Affairs. Unlike the UK, Australia does not publish a simple downloadable list, but you can verify a specific employer's ABN (Australian Business Number) through the ABN Lookup tool and cross-reference with the Department of Home Affairs' guidance at [immi.homeaffairs.gov.au](https://immi.homeaffairs.gov.au).

Sector-specific job boards — particularly in healthcare, engineering and agriculture — often pre-filter for sponsoring employers. Care and support work is an active sponsorship sector; see [Care Assistant Jobs in Australia & NZ with Visa Sponsorship 2026](/care-assistant-jobs-australia-new-zealand-visa-sponsorship/) for a sector-specific view.

### Canada: LMIA-Based Employers

In Canada, most work permit sponsorship flows through the **Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA)** process, administered by Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC). An employer must obtain a positive LMIA before hiring most foreign workers. ESDC publishes LMIA disclosure data that names employers and the occupations for which they received positive assessments — searchable via the Open Government portal at open.canada.ca.

The **Express Entry** system also operates employer-supported streams. Provincial Nominee Programs (PNPs) add another layer, with some provinces maintaining their own lists of eligible occupations and employer requirements. Canadian immigration rules are complex and vary by province; verify details with an ICCRC-regulated (now CICC — the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants) representative.

## How to Cross-Reference Registers With Job Boards

Knowing a company is licensed is only half the job. You still need to find a relevant open vacancy. Here is a practical cross-referencing method.

### Step 1: Build Your Sector-Specific Target List

Filter the UK register (or equivalent) by your occupation sector. For example, a software engineer targeting the UK would filter for technology and IT services companies, then note those in cities where tech employers cluster — London, Manchester, Edinburgh, Bristol.

### Step 2: Search Job Boards With Employer Names

Take your target list and search each employer directly on:

- **LinkedIn Jobs** (filter by company, then add "visa sponsorship" in keywords)
- **Indeed** and **Glassdoor** (employer name + location + role keywords)
- The employer's own careers page (most large sponsors list jobs with relocation and visa details)

LinkedIn introduced a dedicated "visa sponsorship" filter in job search. It is useful but imperfect — not all employers who can sponsor tick the box, and some tick it without having fully thought through the process. Use it as a signal, not a guarantee.

### Step 3: Use Specialist Sponsorship Job Boards

Several aggregator platforms specifically cross-reference the UK register with live postings. These change over time, so evaluate any platform critically: ask whether it pulls directly from the GOV.UK register or relies on employer self-declaration. Self-declaration is weaker — always verify the employer's licence status independently.

### The Healthcare Sector: A Practical Example

Illustrative scenario: A registered nurse qualified in India wants to work in the UK. She downloads the UK Register of Licensed Sponsors and filters for NHS trusts and private hospital groups. She identifies 40 organisations within commuting distance of family in Birmingham. She visits each careers page and finds eight with live Band 5 nursing vacancies. Of those, five explicitly mention visa sponsorship; three do not mention it but hold an active licence. Three invite her to interview.

This illustrative example is not a guaranteed outcome, but it demonstrates that the register-first method surfaces employers the job boards alone would not.

## Approaching Companies That Are Not Yet Licensed Sponsors

Not every good employer has a sponsor licence on day one. Some excellent companies have never needed to hire internationally before, but would be willing to if the right candidate appeared. This is a real opportunity — particularly in sectors with genuine skills shortages.

If you identify a company that interests you but does not appear on the register:

1. **Do not assume they cannot sponsor.** They may be in the process of applying, or the role may not have existed until now.
2. **Make the process easy for them.** Explain that applying for a UK sponsor licence is a documented process — it does not require a lawyer, though one helps. The standard application takes around eight weeks; a priority service exists. The costs are borne by the employer: a licence fee (varying by organisation size), an Immigration Skills Charge, and a Certificate of Sponsorship fee.
3. **Direct them to resources.** The Home Office guidance is detailed. Many employers are simply unfamiliar with the process.

When approaching an employer about sponsorship, tone and timing matter. Read [How to Ask an Employer About Visa Sponsorship (2026)](/how-to-ask-employer-about-visa-sponsorship/) for a structured approach to that conversation.

## Common Mistakes When Searching for Sponsoring Employers

### 1. Relying Only on Job Board Keyword Searches

**The mistake:** Searching "visa sponsorship" on Indeed or LinkedIn and assuming the results are complete and accurate.

**The fix:** Start with the official register, build a target list, then search job boards for those specific employers. The keyword approach misses employers who do not tag their posts and surfaces employers who misuse the phrase.

### 2. Not Verifying the Employer's Licence Is Current

**The mistake:** Assuming a company that sponsored someone's visa two years ago still holds an active licence.

**The fix:** Check the register every time. Licences can be suspended or revoked. The register is the only authoritative source.

### 3. Applying Only to Large, Famous Employers

**The mistake:** Limiting applications to FTSE 100 companies or Fortune 500 firms because they "obviously" sponsor visas.

**The fix:** Small and medium employers are well-represented on the register. In the UK, the majority of sponsors are not household names. Expanding your target list to mid-sized employers in your sector substantially increases your options.

### 4. Accepting Offers That Include Visa Costs Passed to the Worker

**The mistake:** Agreeing to reimburse the employer for the Immigration Skills Charge, Certificate of Sponsorship fee, or visa application fee.

**The fix:** Do not agree to this under any circumstances. In the UK, the Home Office's sponsor guidance explicitly prohibits sponsors from passing the Immigration Skills Charge to the worker. More broadly, no legitimate employer anywhere charges a worker for the privilege of being hired. If this is presented as a condition of the offer, walk away and consider reporting it.

### 5. Conflating "Willing to Sponsor" With "Able to Sponsor"

**The mistake:** Taking an employer's word that they can sponsor without checking the register.

**The fix:** An employer can be genuinely willing but not yet licensed. Willingness and legal ability are different things. Verify on the register. If they are not yet licensed, you can explore whether they would apply — but do not accept a job offer contingent on a future licence being granted without understanding the timeline and risk.

### 6. Ignoring the Salary Threshold

**The mistake:** Applying for roles where the salary is below the going rate for the visa route.

**The fix:** For the UK Skilled Worker visa, there is a minimum salary threshold that must be met — both a general floor and a going rate for the specific occupation code. These figures are set by the Home Office and are updated; verify the current thresholds at GOV.UK. If the advertised salary is below the threshold, the employer cannot sponsor you for that role without paying more. Knowing this before you apply saves everyone's time.

## Using Recruitment Agencies and Immigration Lawyers as Intermediaries

Some recruitment agencies specialise in placing internationally-mobile candidates and work exclusively with licensed sponsors. These can be efficient, but exercise caution:

- A legitimate recruitment agency is paid by the employer, never by the candidate.
- If an agency charges you a registration fee, placement fee, or "processing fee" in connection with finding you a sponsored job, that is a red flag.
- Agencies cannot guarantee visa outcomes.

Immigration solicitors and OISC-regulated advisers can help you assess whether a particular employer and role would likely meet the eligibility criteria for your visa route — but they are not job-finding services. Where your individual eligibility or a specific employer's compliance posture is genuinely in question, a qualified immigration adviser is the right call. We do not provide immigration legal advice on this site.

A Bulgarian-born forklift operator holds a European licence and wants to work in Germany. Germany's immigration framework since the Skilled Immigration Act 2020 (amended) allows non-EU nationals to work in regulated vocational occupations if their qualification is recognised. He researches German logistics companies using the Federal Employment Agency's (Bundesagentur für Arbeit) job portal, Make it in Germany (make-it-in-germany.com), and German-language job boards such as StepStone.de. He also contacts the relevant trade association for logistics and warehousing in Germany to ask which member companies regularly recruit from outside the EU. His qualification recognition application to the relevant German authority runs in parallel with his job search — a common approach since recognition can take months. ## Keeping Your Search Organised

Once you are operating across multiple employers and countries, tracking becomes important. A simple spreadsheet with the following columns prevents you from losing good leads:

- Employer name
- Country and city
- Licence verified? (Yes / No / Not applicable)
- Date licence verified
- Open vacancy found? (Role title and link)
- Application status
- Next action and date

Review the list weekly. Licences can be added and removed; roles close. A candidate who checks back after an initial rejection six months later sometimes finds the employer is hiring again.

## What Happens After You Find a Sponsoring Employer

Finding the employer is the beginning of the process, not the end. Once you have a job offer from a licensed sponsor, the employer must issue you a **Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS)** — a reference number that you use in your visa application. The CoS confirms the job details, salary, and occupation code. For a full walkthrough of what comes next, see [Visa Sponsorship Application Process End to End (2026)](/visa-sponsorship-application-process-end-to-end/).

The employer has ongoing compliance duties too: reporting changes in your employment status, conducting right to work checks, and maintaining records. If you are curious about what your employer is obligated to do, that context can be useful in conversations with them.

## A Final Word on Scams and Exploitation

The demand for visa sponsorship creates conditions that bad actors exploit. Common scams include:

- Fake job adverts that collect personal and financial information under the guise of a visa application
- "Agents" who charge large fees to place you with a sponsor, then disappear
- Employers who use the visa process to create dependency and exploit workers

Protect yourself with three rules: verify the employer on the official register, never pay for a job or a visa sponsorship, and if something feels wrong, stop and seek independent advice. In the UK, the GLAA (Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority) investigates labour exploitation and has a confidential reporting line.

Finding a genuine sponsor takes research and patience. The tools are publicly available, the registers are real, and thousands of employers sponsor overseas workers every year. The job seekers who succeed are typically those who approach the search systematically — using the official tools, targeting the right sectors, and presenting themselves as candidates who understand the process as well as the employer does.
