You have the job. You have the visa. You land, and within a week you discover that almost everything in the United States requires a bank account — and that the first branch you walk into tells you that you need a Social Security number before they can open one.

That sentence costs people a great deal of money. Those who accept it spend months cashing wages at storefronts taking two or three per cent off the top, carry rent in cash, and send money home through the most expensive channel available because they have no account to send it from.

It is also not what federal law says.

## The regulation

Banks operate a Customer Identification Program under rules made following the USA PATRIOT Act. The provision is **31 CFR 1020.220**, and the identification requirement sits at paragraph (a)(2)(i)(A).

Before opening an account a bank must obtain four things: your name, date of birth, address, and an identification number.

The rule then distinguishes between two classes of customer.

For a **US person**, the identification number must be a taxpayer identification number.

For a **non-US person**, the bank may obtain and record **any one** of the following:

- a taxpayer identification number;
- **passport number and country of issuance**;
- alien identification card number; or
- the number and country of issuance of any other government-issued document evidencing nationality or residence, bearing a photograph or similar safeguard.

The passport provision is an alternative, not an addition. A bank that identifies a non-US person by passport number and country of issuance has satisfied the rule completely.

## So why does every branch ask?

Because the regulation is a floor, not a ceiling. Banks may be stricter, and most are, for reasons that are commercial rather than legal.

**Credit products.** If the account bundles a credit card, overdraft or loan, the bank wants to pull a credit file, and the bureaus key on an SSN or ITIN. A plain checking account with no credit attached raises none of this — but the policy was often written once and never revisited.

**Interest reporting.** An account paying interest creates an IRS reporting obligation requiring a taxpayer identification number. A non-interest-bearing checking account creates no such obligation. This is why some banks will open you a checking account on a passport but refuse a savings account, and why asking specifically for a non-interest-bearing account sometimes resolves the whole conversation.

**Mandatory software fields.** Many account-opening systems were specified when every customer was assumed to have an SSN. The field will not accept a blank and the person serving you cannot override it. They are not inventing an obstacle.

**Risk appetite.** Some institutions have decided this customer segment is more compliance work than it is worth.

None of these is the law. All of them will stop you at a particular branch. The conclusion is that this is a shopping problem, not an argument to win.

## The ITIN

An Individual Taxpayer Identification Number is issued by the IRS to people with a US tax obligation who cannot get an SSN. Nine digits, formatted like an SSN, always starting with a 9.

It is not work authorisation, not immigration status, and applying for one does not place you on any immigration list — the IRS and immigration enforcement are separate agencies.

You need one when you have US income to report and no SSN. You do not need one to hold a checking account, but having one widens your options considerably because it satisfies the "taxpayer identification number" limb and slots into the bank's existing software without the mandatory-field problem.

The order that works:

1. **Open a basic account on your passport now**, wherever one will be opened.
2. **Apply for the ITIN when you have a filing requirement**, on Form W-7 with your return, or through an IRS-authorised Certifying Acceptance Agent if you would rather not post your passport.
3. **Add the ITIN** when it arrives. Savings products and sometimes a secured card open up at that point.

Note that if you arrived on a sponsored work visa you will usually be eligible for an SSN itself, which makes all of this simpler. Apply for it as soon as you are authorised to work — the ITIN route is for people who are not eligible.

## Documents that work

Bring more than you think you need. A branch that would have refused one document frequently proceeds with four on the counter.

| Document | What it does |
|---|---|
| Passport, unexpired | Satisfies the identification rule directly |
| Second photo ID (national ID, consular card, foreign licence) | Fills a "one primary, one secondary" policy |
| Proof of address (lease, utility bill, employer letter) | Satisfies the address element |
| Visa, I-94, EAD or I-20 | Answers the unspoken question about how long you will be here |
| ITIN or SSN letter, if held | Removes the software problem entirely |

On the address element: the rule requires a residential or business street address, and a post office box alone will not do. There is a provision permitting the address of a next of kin or another contact for a person with no residential address — but you need a branch that knows it exists.

## Which institutions say yes

Naming banks is unhelpful because policies change quietly and vary between branches of the same bank. The categories are durable.

**Large national banks.** Mixed. Several will open accounts on a passport plus a second document, and branches in areas with large immigrant populations usually have staff who have done it many times. The same bank will refuse you twenty miles away. If refused, try another branch before writing off the bank.

**Community banks and credit unions.** Often the best option, particularly those with a stated community development mission. Credit union membership is usually easier to obtain than people expect, and decisions are made locally, so a manager who understands the rule can simply approve it.

**Banks advertising in your language.** Signage or a website in your language means the bank has a process for customers like you. That is a commercial decision, not an accident.

**Online banks and fintech apps.** Often fastest, but read the terms. Many fintech products are not banks — they are programmes on a partner bank's licence, and deposit protection passes through that partner. Some require an SSN at sign-up regardless, because onboarding is automated and the identity vendor is keyed to one.

## What to say when refused

**Ask what specifically is missing.** "We need a Social Security number" and "our system requires a TIN for an interest-bearing account" are different statements with different solutions.

**Ask for a non-interest-bearing checking account.** This removes the IRS reporting trigger.

**Ask for the manager.** Tellers work from summaries; managers can see the full Customer Identification Program document and its exceptions.

**Ask the precise question.** "Does your CIP permit a passport number and country of issuance for a non-US person?" It is answerable from the bank's own documents.

**Then go elsewhere.** This is a search, not an appeal. People routinely succeed at the fourth institution with the identical documents refused at the first three.

**Ask your employer.** If you arrived on a sponsored role, your employer may already have a relationship with a bank that handles international hires routinely. HR teams at organisations that sponsor regularly often know exactly which branch to send you to, and nobody ever asks.

## Building a credit file

An account is a gateway, not a destination. Without a credit file you will be refused apartments, phone contracts and car loans, and asked for deposits that assume the worst.

**A secured credit card** is the standard route. Deposit a sum, receive a card with a matching limit, put one predictable bill on it and pay in full monthly. After six to twelve months a file exists. Check the issuer reports to all three bureaus — some report to one, which does very little.

**A credit-builder loan** from a credit union lends a small sum into a locked savings account; you repay in instalments, the repayments are reported, and at the end you receive the money.

**Rent reporting services** report rent to the bureaus for a fee. Worth it if you pay reliably and have nothing else on file.

A file keyed to an ITIN works but is thinner than one keyed to an SSN. If you later obtain an SSN, ask the bureaus to merge the histories rather than starting again.

## Sending money home

Once an account exists, remittance costs fall sharply.

A wire from a branch is usually the most expensive way to move money. Transfer services that debit a US account and pay out in local currency are generally far cheaper, and the real cost is in the exchange rate margin rather than the advertised fee. The only comparison that means anything is the amount that actually arrives.

For regular transfers to the same recipient, set up a recurring transfer — many services price those below one-off sends. And check whether the receiving country caps cash payouts, because a transfer that arrives but cannot be collected is worse than one that never left.

Keep records. If you are supporting family abroad, the transfers may matter later for tax, for a visa application evidencing genuine family ties, or for a sponsor income calculation.

## Business accounts

A US entity needs an Employer Identification Number from the IRS. Non-residents can obtain one without an SSN by filing Form SS-4, through a specific process for applicants with no US taxpayer identification number.

With an EIN and formation documents a business account becomes possible, but banks also apply beneficial ownership rules, which means identifying and verifying the individuals who own or control the entity — and those individuals fall under the identification requirements above, where a passport again does the work.

In practice, business accounts for non-resident-owned entities are harder than personal accounts. Many banks require an in-person visit by a controlling person; some will not open them at all.

## Joint accounts

A joint account with a spouse who holds an SSN is frequently the path of least resistance, because the bank runs its normal process on one holder and applies the non-US person provisions to the other.

Understand what you are agreeing to. A joint account is generally owned by both people, either can withdraw the entire balance, and it can be exposed to either holder's creditors. For spouses that is often acceptable. For a friend or flatmate, it rarely is.

## What changes when your status changes

If you later obtain an SSN, add it to your existing accounts rather than opening new ones. Account history has value, and a long-standing account is treated differently when you apply for credit. Ask the bureaus to merge any ITIN-based file with the new SSN file.

If your immigration documents expire, expect a request to update them. Banks run periodic reviews and a lapsed passport on file can trigger a restriction. Supplying a current document usually resolves it within days.

If you leave the United States, decide deliberately whether to close accounts. Keeping one open is useful if you may return, but a dormant account can attract fees and, in some states, escheat to the state as unclaimed property after a period of inactivity.

## What this page will not tell you

There is no official list of which banks accept passports, because no regulator maintains one. There is no reliable current figure for how many immigrants are unbanked. And there is no average time to open an account, because it ranges from twenty minutes to several weeks depending on which door you walk through.

The regulation is the fixed point.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to be a citizen to open a US bank account?**
No. The rule expressly contemplates non-US persons and lists the identifiers available for them.

**Is an SSN legally required?**
Not for a non-US person. Individual banks may require one as policy, which is different.

**I am here on a sponsored work visa — should I get an SSN or an ITIN?**
An SSN. If you are authorised to work you are generally eligible for one, and it is simpler than an ITIN in every respect.

**Can I open an account before arriving?**
Sometimes, through an international bank where you are already a customer. Most US banks require you to be present.

**Is an ITIN a work permit?**
No. It is issued by the IRS for tax purposes only.

**Can I get a debit card without an SSN?**
Yes. A debit card draws on your own deposits and involves no credit check.

**What if my account is closed later?**
Ask in writing for the reason and the policy relied on. Accounts are often closed during periodic reviews when documents expire.

**Will opening an account affect my immigration case?**
Opening a bank account is an ordinary financial act. Concerns specific to your case belong with an immigration attorney, not a bank.

## A note on supervisory attention

Two developments explain why some branches have become more cautious recently.

FinCEN issued an order in August 2025 addressing taxpayer identification number collection at account opening, and in June 2026 published an advisory concerning non-work-authorised populations.

The effect on an ordinary customer is indirect but real: compliance functions have been paying closer attention to this category of account. Some banks have become more conservative; others more careful to document that they are following the rule correctly.

Neither changes 31 CFR 1020.220. The passport provision remains in force and says what it says. If you are refused and the reason given is vague, it may simply be that the branch has had recent internal guidance and is applying it conservatively — which is another reason to try elsewhere rather than argue.
