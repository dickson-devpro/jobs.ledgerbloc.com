*Legal points on this page were checked on 22 November 2026 against HUD. Market figures are landlord convention, not law, and are flagged as such.*

You have the visa and the job. You land, and discover that renting an apartment requires three things you have had no opportunity to acquire: a US credit score, a Social Security number, and a US rental history.

This is not a wall. Landlords rent to new arrivals constantly, particularly in cities with large employers who sponsor. But the screening process was built around domestic applicants, and if you walk into it unprepared you will be rejected by software before a human sees your file.

The fix is to arrive with a package that answers the questions the credit file would have answered.

## What landlords are actually screening for

Three things, in roughly this order.

**Credit.** A score, pulled from Equifax, Experian or TransUnion. A new arrival has no file at all — which, perversely, can be treated worse than a poor one, because automated systems are built to sort scores rather than to handle their absence. Corporate-managed buildings commonly set a minimum in the low-to-mid 600s. That number is a company policy, not a legal threshold, and it varies.

**Income.** The convention is that gross monthly income should be two and a half to three times the monthly rent. On a $2,000 apartment that means $5,000 to $6,000 a month. Again: convention, not law, and negotiable in ways the credit requirement usually is not.

**Rental history.** Previous landlords who will confirm you paid on time. Overseas landlords count, if you can produce them in a form a US leasing agent can read.

For someone arriving on a sponsored role, the second of these is your strongest card and the one most people underplay. You have a signed employment contract with a named employer, a start date and a stated salary. That is better evidence of ability to pay than a credit score, and a human decision-maker will usually accept it. The problem is getting to a human.

## SSN, ITIN, or neither

A Social Security number is issued to people authorised to work. If you have arrived on a work visa you are generally eligible, and you should apply in your first week — not because the apartment needs it, but because your employer's payroll does, and because an emergency tax code will shrink your first payslips until it arrives.

An **Individual Taxpayer Identification Number** is an IRS-issued number for people with a US tax obligation who cannot get an SSN. Nine digits, beginning with 9. Many landlords accept it.

And a substantial number of private landlords will accept a **passport number** alone, because what they are actually using the number for is a background check, and the check can run on other identifiers.

The practical sequence: apply for the SSN immediately, start the apartment search without waiting for it, and have the passport and visa documents ready as the substitute. Waiting for the card before you start looking costs you weeks you do not have.

## Build the file before you view anything

Assemble this before the first viewing. An applicant who produces a complete package on the spot beats one with better credit who says they will email it later.

| Document | What it does | If you do not have it |
|---|---|---|
| Passport and visa | Primary identity | — |
| I-94 record | Proves lawful entry; free from cbp.gov | Print the visa and entry stamp |
| Employment letter | Salary, start date, role, on letterhead | The signed contract |
| SSN or ITIN | Runs the background check | Passport number |
| Bank statements, 3–6 months | Shows reserves | Overseas statements plus a translation |
| Foreign credit report | Evidence of a payment record | A reference letter from your home bank |
| Previous landlord reference | Rental history | Letter from a mortgage lender, if you owned |
| Co-signer agreement | Fallback for thin files | Offer a larger deposit instead |

Two things worth adding that most guides omit.

**A one-page cover sheet.** Who you are, your employer, your role and start date, your salary, what you are offering, and a list of what is attached. Leasing agents process dozens of applications; the one that explains itself gets read.

**Translations.** An overseas bank statement in another language is not evidence to someone who cannot read it. A plain translation, even an informal one, converts a confusing document into a persuasive one.

If your home country's credit history can be translated into a US-readable report, that is worth doing — services exist for several countries, though coverage is patchy and depends on where you are coming from.

## What it costs up front

This is where arrivals get caught, because the cash goes out before any salary comes in.

A realistic US apartment at **$2,000 a month** typically requires:

| Item | Typical |
|---|---|
| First month's rent | $2,000 |
| Security deposit | One to two months' rent |
| Last month's rent, where required | $2,000 |
| Application fee, per adult | $30–$75 |
| Utility connection deposits | A few hundred |
| Renter's insurance, annual | $150–$300 |
| Moving and basic furnishing | Highly variable |

For a single applicant on a mid-priced apartment that totals roughly **$6,000 to $8,000** before furniture. In high-cost metros — New York, San Francisco, Boston — the same arithmetic on a $4,000 apartment reaches **$12,000 to $16,000**.

Those are illustrative ranges built from the components above, not a published statistic. Nobody publishes a reliable national average for this, and figures that circulate as though they were surveyed numbers are usually somebody's estimate repeated until it hardened.

**Security deposits are capped by law in many states** — some limit them to one month's rent, some to two, some not at all. Check your specific state before accepting that a demanded deposit is normal.

For a sponsored worker, the important point is the timing. You will pay this in your first fortnight and receive your first full payslip four to six weeks later. If your employer offers a relocation allowance, ask whether any of it can be advanced rather than reimbursed. Reimbursement arrives after you have already needed the money.

## What you can actually negotiate

More than people assume, and the leverage is highest on a unit that has been listed a while.

**The deposit**, where state law permits variation — a larger deposit is frequently accepted in exchange for a thin credit file, and offering it proactively reframes you from risky to prepared.

**Start date.** Landlords price empty months badly. A mid-month start you can accommodate is worth real money.

**Lease length.** Longer terms attract concessions; shorter ones cost a premium. If your visa status is secure, a longer lease is usually cheaper per month.

**An early termination clause.** Negotiate this at signing, not later. For anyone on employer-tied permission, this is the single most valuable clause in the document — see below.

**Pet and parking fees**, which are frequently padded and frequently reducible.

**Concessions over rent reductions.** A landlord who will not drop the headline rent will often give a free month, waive the application fee or throw in parking, because those do not reset the building's rent benchmark.

## The clause that matters most for sponsored workers

Your permission to be in the country is tied to your employer. If that role ends, you have a limited window to find another sponsor or leave — and it is short.

A twelve-month lease with no early termination clause means that in that scenario you are liable for the remaining rent on an apartment in a country you may be required to leave.

Negotiate an early termination provision at signing. The usual shape is sixty days' notice plus a penalty of one to two months' rent. That is not cheap, but it is bounded, and the alternative is unbounded.

If the landlord will not include one, ask for a shorter initial term instead. A six-month lease at a slightly higher rate is cheaper than a twelve-month lease you cannot exit.

## Reading the lease

Take 24 to 48 hours. A landlord who will not allow that is telling you something.

**Rent increases.** Can the rent change mid-term, and on what notice?

**Late fees.** Many states cap these, commonly around 5% of rent or a flat amount. A clause above the state cap is unenforceable but will still be charged until challenged.

**Entry rights.** Notice before the landlord enters is typically 24 to 48 hours in states that regulate it. A lease giving unrestricted entry should be queried.

**Repairs.** Which repairs are yours and which are the landlord's, and what the response time is.

**Subletting.** Relevant if your employer might relocate you.

**Auto-renewal.** Many leases renew automatically unless you give notice 60 to 90 days ahead. Put that date in your calendar the day you sign, because missing it commits you to another full term.

## Your rights

Two things every new arrival should know.

**The Fair Housing Act protects national origin.** HUD lists the protected classes as race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status and disability. A landlord may not refuse to rent to you, impose different terms, or steer you to particular buildings because of where you are from. If that happens, you can file a complaint with HUD.

A landlord asking for documents that establish identity and ability to pay is doing something ordinary. A landlord treating you differently because of your nationality is not, and the distinction matters.

**Habitability and deposits.** Landlords must maintain the property in liveable condition — the standard varies by state. Deposits must be returned within a defined period after move-out, commonly 14 to 30 days, with an itemised statement of deductions. Several states impose multiple-damages penalties on landlords who withhold wrongly.

Retaliation for exercising these rights — complaining about repairs, contacting a housing authority — is prohibited in most states.

**Document the condition on move-in.** Photograph and video every room before you put anything in it, timestamped, and send a copy to the landlord in writing the same day. This is the cheapest insurance available and it is what decides deposit disputes.

## Temporary housing first is often the right call

Signing a twelve-month lease on an apartment you found online, in a neighbourhood you have never stood in, in your first week, is a decision made with the least information you will ever have.

One to three months of furnished short-term housing costs more per month and is frequently worth it. You get a US address for your bank account and SSN application, time to learn which neighbourhoods actually suit your commute, and a position of strength rather than urgency when you do sign.

If your employer provides temporary accommodation as part of relocation, take it even if you were planning to find somewhere permanent immediately.

## If you are self-employed or run a business

Different evidence, same principle.

Bank statements over six months rather than three. A profit and loss statement, ideally prepared by an accountant. Business registration documents. Tax returns from your home jurisdiction. Client contracts showing forward revenue.

Expect more friction, and offer a larger deposit or a co-signer pre-emptively rather than waiting to be asked.

## Build credit from the first month

Every month you rent without a credit file is a month wasted, and a thin file is what will make your next apartment, your car and your phone contract expensive.

**Rent reporting services** will report your payments to the bureaus for a monthly fee. Worth it while you have nothing else reporting.

**A secured credit card.** Deposit a few hundred dollars, receive a card with a matching limit, put one small recurring bill on it, pay in full every month. Check it reports to all three bureaus — some report to one, which does very little.

**A credit-builder loan** from a credit union lends a small sum into a locked account, you repay in instalments, and the repayments are reported.

**Authorised user status** on a spouse's established card transfers some of that history to your file.

A file typically exists after about six months of reported activity. A score good enough to get you decent terms usually takes one to two years.

## A timeline

| When | What |
|---|---|
| 8–12 weeks out | Assemble documents; request landlord references; translate statements |
| 4–6 weeks out | Research neighbourhoods; book temporary housing; budget the full upfront cash |
| Week 1 | Apply for SSN; open a bank account; get a US phone number |
| Weeks 2–4 | View in person; apply with the complete package; negotiate |
| Signing | Read over 48 hours; secure an early termination clause; diarise the renewal date |
| Move-in | Photograph everything; send to landlord in writing; set up renter's insurance |

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I rent without a Social Security number?**
Yes. Many landlords accept an ITIN or a passport number. Apply for the SSN anyway — your payroll needs it.

**Do I need a US credit score?**
No landlord is legally required to demand one, and many accept employment and income evidence instead. Corporate-managed buildings are the strictest; private landlords are usually more flexible.

**How much cash should I arrive with?**
Enough for first month, deposit, possibly last month, fees, utility deposits and two months of living costs. On a mid-priced apartment that is realistically $8,000 to $10,000 including a cushion.

**Can a landlord refuse me because of my nationality?**
No. National origin is a protected class under the Fair Housing Act. Requesting documents is lawful; treating you differently because of where you are from is not.

**Will I need a co-signer?**
Sometimes, for a thin credit file. A larger deposit is often accepted as an alternative, and is easier than finding a US guarantor you do not have.

**What if my job ends during the lease?**
This is why the early termination clause matters. Without one you remain liable for the full term. Negotiate it at signing.

**Is renting different from Europe?**
Yes. Deposits are usually larger, leases are typically fixed twelve-month terms rather than rolling, unfurnished genuinely means no appliances in some markets, and renter's insurance is commonly mandatory.

**When do I get my deposit back?**
Commonly 14 to 30 days after move-out depending on state, with an itemised list of deductions. Your move-in photographs are what settle any dispute.
