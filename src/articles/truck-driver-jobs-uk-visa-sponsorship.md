## Can Truck Drivers Get UK Visa Sponsorship in 2026?

Yes — heavy goods vehicle (HGV) and large goods vehicle (LGV) driving roles are eligible for the UK Skilled Worker visa, provided the employer holds a Home Office sponsor licence and is willing to issue a Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS). The route is not automatic, not available through any third party, and not something you can self-initiate. Every step begins with the employer. If you understand that constraint from the outset, the rest of the process becomes much more manageable.

The UK has experienced a persistent structural shortage of qualified HGV drivers since well before Brexit, and that shortage has shaped how seriously the haulage and logistics sector now treats international recruitment. For the right candidate with the right licence and the right cargo experience, visa-sponsored roles do exist and continue to be filled — but the licensing and salary compliance hurdles are higher than many candidates realise.

---

## What the Job Actually Involves, and Why the Shortage Persists

Driving a truck in the UK is not a single job description. The role varies substantially depending on the licence category held, the cargo type, the employer's operation, and the route pattern.

A **Category C (rigid vehicle)** driver typically operates a two-axle lorry, often on local or regional distribution runs, making multiple drops per shift. Think supermarket supply chains, building materials, or general palletised freight. Shift patterns are often early starts — 04:00 departures are standard — and the physical demands include manual handling, pallet truck operation, tail-lift work and adherence to strict delivery windows.

A **Category C+E (articulated vehicle)** driver operates an artic or road train, often on longer trunk routes between distribution centres or ports. These roles carry higher pay and greater responsibility: reversing 44-tonne combinations into tight bays, managing tachograph compliance, completing driver daily walkaround checks, and communicating defects through the employer's transport management system.

Specialist categories include tanker driving (ADR licence required for hazardous goods), temperature-controlled (refrigerated trailer or chilled distribution), car transporter, and abnormal load escort. Each of these commands a premium and narrows the pool of eligible candidates further.

**Why the shortage is structural, not cyclical:** The UK's driver demographic skews old. A significant proportion of the existing driver workforce is over 55, and attrition through retirement is running ahead of new entrant training. The HGV licence acquisition process is expensive — training costs can run to several thousand pounds — and the lifestyle is not universally appealing to domestic candidates: unsocial hours, time away from home, and physical demands at both ends of the journey. Brexit removed the pool of EU drivers who had been filling the gap informally for years. The result is that employers in haulage, food logistics, and bulk materials have turned to international recruitment in a way they had not historically needed to.

## What It Pays

Truck driver salaries in the UK are quoted in two ways: **annualised salary** for salaried permanent roles, and **hourly rates** for agency or variable-hours employment. Both matter because the Skilled Worker visa requires the job offer to meet a minimum salary threshold — verify the current figure on [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/skilled-worker-visa/your-job) — and the way pay is structured can affect visa compliance.

Indicative annual salary ranges for sponsored roles, based on typical advertised figures in the sector:

| Role type | Indicative annual range | What moves it higher |
|---|---|---|
| Category C rigid, regional distribution | £30,000–£36,000 | Night or weekend premium, HIAB attachment |
| Category C+E artic, trunk routes | £34,000–£42,000 | Tramping (away from home), mileage volume |
| Tanker driver (non-hazardous) | £36,000–£44,000 | Product specialism, shift antisociability |
| ADR tanker (hazardous goods) | £40,000–£48,000+ | Dangerous goods categories carried |
| Temperature-controlled distribution | £32,000–£40,000 | Night work, double-manned trunking |

These ranges are indicative and vary significantly by region. London, the South East and major distribution hubs near ports tend to pay more than rural areas. Night-shift differentials, bank holiday rates, and overtime can push take-home earnings substantially above the headline salary.

**Critical point on visa compliance:** The Skilled Worker route requires your salary to meet both the general minimum threshold and the specific going rate for the SOC code covering your role, whichever is higher. These figures were updated in 2024. Do not assume a job offer at a figure that sounded compliant a year ago is still compliant today — check the current thresholds on gov.uk before accepting or asking an employer to proceed.

## The Visa Route in Detail

The visa that covers truck driving is the **Skilled Worker visa**, administered by UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI), which sits within the Home Office. There is no sector-specific sub-route for transport workers equivalent to the Health and Care Worker route.

### Step 1: The employer checks their sponsor licence

The employer must already hold a Home Office sponsor licence, or apply for one. Applying for a licence takes time — typically several weeks for a straightforward application — and the Home Office can request an audit visit before granting it. If the employer does not yet have a licence, factor that into your timeline.

### Step 2: The employer issues a Certificate of Sponsorship

Once licensed, the employer assigns you a CoS through the Sponsor Management System. The CoS is not a physical document — it is a unique reference number tied to the specific job offer, salary, and start date. It is valid for three months and must be used promptly.

### Step 3: You apply for the visa

Using the CoS reference number, you submit your Skilled Worker visa application online through UKVI. You will need to provide:

- A valid passport
- Your CoS reference number
- Proof that your salary meets the threshold (your job offer letter or contract)
- English language evidence (unless exempt by nationality or prior qualification)
- Proof of personal savings to support yourself on arrival, unless your employer certifies they will meet this requirement
- Any relevant licensing documents (discussed in the next section)

You pay the visa application fee and the Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS) at the point of application. These are substantial costs — check the current fee schedule on gov.uk. Your employer pays the Immigration Skills Charge, which is a separate levy charged to the company for each sponsored worker.

### Step 4: Biometrics and decision

You attend a visa application centre in your country of residence to submit biometric data. Processing times vary by country and by time of year. Priority services are available at additional cost in some locations. Standard processing is typically around three weeks from biometrics submission, but this is not guaranteed.

## Qualifications and Licensing — The Critical Hurdle

This section is where many international truck driver applicants underestimate the complexity. Holding a valid HGV licence in your home country does not mean you can drive an HGV in the UK the day you arrive.

### Licence categories

UK driving licences use a specific category system. Category C covers rigid lorries over 3.5 tonnes. Category C+E covers articulated vehicles and road trains. You need to identify which UK category your overseas licence maps to.

### EU and EEA licence holders

If your driving licence was issued in an EU or EEA member state, you can exchange it for a UK equivalent through the DVLA without retaking a test in most cases, for the corresponding category. However, this exchange must be completed within a specified period after you take up UK residence, and there are restrictions on driving commercially before the exchange is processed. The DVLA exchange process for LGV categories can take longer than the standard car licence exchange — allow for this in your timeline.

### Non-EU/EEA licence holders

If your licence was issued outside the EU/EEA — for example, in India, the Philippines, South Africa, or Australia — you will almost certainly need to sit the full UK LGV theory and practical test series. This includes the LGV theory test, the hazard perception test, and the Category C or C+E practical driving test conducted through the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA). Some employers will agree to begin your sponsorship process with a provisional UK licence and support you through testing after arrival; others will only proceed once you hold a full UK entitlement. Clarify this before accepting any offer.

### Driver Certificate of Professional Competence (CPC)

All professional LGV drivers operating commercially in the UK must hold a valid Driver CPC. This is separate from the driving licence. The initial CPC qualification involves two theory modules and two practical modules. Existing EU-issued CPCs may be exchangeable, but non-EU CPCs are generally not recognised, and you would need to complete the full UK qualification. Periodic CPC training (35 hours every five years) is then required to maintain the qualification.

### ADR (Hazardous Goods)

If the role involves carrying dangerous goods by road — fuel tankers, chemical tankers, or gas distribution — you need a valid ADR certificate for the relevant class of goods. ADR certificates are issued after approved training and examination and are internationally recognised under the UN's European Agreement framework, so an overseas ADR certificate may be accepted or reduce the training required. Confirm the specific class requirements with the employer.

## Which Employers Actually Sponsor Truck Drivers

Not every haulage company or logistics operator will sponsor overseas drivers. The cost and administrative burden of holding a sponsor licence mean that sponsorship tends to concentrate in larger operations. Understanding who actually does it helps you focus your search.

**Large logistics and distribution companies** — businesses operating national distribution networks with dozens or hundreds of trucks are the most likely to maintain a sponsor licence. They have dedicated HR or compliance teams, high vacancy volumes, and the scale to absorb the sponsor licence fee and immigration skills charge.

**Food and drink manufacturers with own-account fleets** — major food producers, dairy companies, and drink distributors often run their own transport operations. They employ drivers directly rather than through third-party hauliers and are more likely to sponsor because their transport function is integrated with the business.

**Bulk hauliers** — companies carrying aggregates, waste, grain, or other bulk commodities often face specific driver shortages because the work requires specialist tipping trailer or walking floor experience. The niche skill set makes international recruitment more attractive.

**Tanker operators** — particularly those carrying fuel, chemicals, or food-grade liquid bulk. These operators already deal with regulatory complexity (ADR, food safety audits, operator licence conditions) and are generally more comfortable navigating immigration compliance as well.

**Temporary staffing agencies with their own sponsor licences** — several large transport recruitment agencies employ drivers directly on PAYE and hold sponsor licences. They place workers with client companies but the employment relationship is with the agency. This is legal and common. The key check: confirm the agency itself holds the licence and will appear on your CoS as the employer.

**How to verify a sponsor:** The Home Office publishes a Register of Licensed Sponsors, updated regularly and freely searchable on gov.uk. Before progressing any application, search for the company by name on that register. If they do not appear, they cannot legally issue you a CoS, regardless of what they claim.

## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected — And How to Avoid Them

1. **The employer is not on the sponsor register.** Fix: Check the register before you invest time in any application. Do not rely on the employer's word alone.

2. **The salary offer falls below the going rate threshold.** Fix: Ask the employer explicitly whether the salary has been checked against the current UKVI going rate for the specific SOC code. If they cannot confirm this, ask them to check before you proceed.

3. **English language evidence is missing or insufficient.** Fix: Understand which approved tests or qualifications UKVI accepts. A letter from a previous employer is not acceptable evidence.

4. **The CoS expires before the visa application is submitted.** Fix: Apply promptly once the CoS is issued. Do not wait. The reference number has a three-month window from issue.

5. **Assuming your overseas licence is immediately valid for commercial driving.** Fix: Contact the DVLA before arrival to understand the exchange process for your specific country of issue and licence category. Build this into your timeline.

6. **Applying without a Driver CPC or assuming it transfers automatically.** Fix: Contact the DVSA to confirm whether your existing CPC qualification is recognised before accepting a role that requires immediate commercial driving.

7. **Using an immigration agent who claims to arrange a job and a visa without an employer.** Fix: This is not how the system works and is likely a scam. The CoS can only be issued by the employer. No agent can substitute for that step.

8. **Accepting a job offer that includes a deduction for visa costs.** Fix: Walk away. Visa-related deductions from a sponsored worker's salary are unlawful under UK law and the Home Office takes them seriously.

## Scam Warning: How to Protect Yourself

The combination of a genuine driver shortage and high international demand for UK visas makes this sector a target for fraudulent job offers. Apply the following checks without exception.

**No legitimate employer or agent charges you to apply for a job or to be sponsored.** The employer bears the Immigration Skills Charge and the CoS fee. If anyone asks you to transfer money to secure a job offer, a CoS reference number, or a visa, you are being defrauded.

**Verify the sponsor licence independently.** Search the government's Register of Licensed Sponsors at gov.uk. It is free, publicly accessible, and updated regularly. If the company name does not appear, they cannot sponsor you, regardless of any documents they produce.

**Verify the company exists and operates legally.** Check Companies House (companieshouse.gov.uk) to confirm the company is registered and active. Look for a physical address, a working website, and a presence on industry directories. Be sceptical of companies that only communicate via WhatsApp or personal email addresses.

**Be cautious of unsolicited approaches.** Genuine sponsors advertise on mainstream job boards (Indeed, Reed, TotalJobs, specialist transport boards such as Logistics UK's job board) or work through reputable agencies. If a recruiter contacts you out of nowhere via social media promising guaranteed sponsorship, apply additional scrutiny.

**Understand what a CoS actually is.** It is a reference number generated inside the Home Office's Sponsor Management System. It cannot be sold, transferred, or created by anyone other than a licensed sponsor employer. Anyone offering to sell you a CoS or a sponsor letter for a fee is committing fraud.

**Report suspected fraud.** If you believe you have encountered a fraudulent sponsor or agent, report it to Action Fraud (actionfraud.police.uk) in the UK, or to the Home Office directly. If you are outside the UK, contact the British Embassy or High Commission in your country.
