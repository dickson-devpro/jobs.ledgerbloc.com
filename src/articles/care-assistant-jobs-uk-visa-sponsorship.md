## Can Care Assistants Get Visa Sponsorship in the UK?

Yes — care assistant and home carer roles are explicitly listed among the eligible occupations for the Skilled Worker route's Health and Care Worker sub-route, which means a UK employer with a valid sponsor licence can legally sponsor you for this position. This is one of the few non-degree-level jobs in the UK where visa sponsorship is a realistic and well-established pathway rather than an exception. Indicative salaries run from roughly £20,000 to £27,000 a year, depending on employer type, region, and shift pattern, though you must check the Home Office's current going-rate threshold before assuming any salary qualifies.

---

## What the Job Actually Involves — and Why Employers Cannot Fill It Domestically

### The day-to-day reality

A care assistant in the UK provides personal, practical and emotional support to people who cannot fully care for themselves — typically elderly people, individuals with disabilities, or people living with long-term conditions such as dementia. The work varies significantly depending on the setting.

In a **residential care home**, you will follow a rota covering morning and evening personal care routines (washing, dressing, continence care), assisting with meals, supporting mobility, and spending time with residents who may have very limited social contact. Night shifts are common and can involve checking on residents and responding to call systems.

In **domiciliary or home care**, you travel between service users' homes in a set area — often on a zero-hours or part-time contract that is gradually extended — providing the same types of personal care but in private settings. This role requires more independence because there is no on-site manager. You write records on each visit, sometimes using a care app on a smartphone, and raise safeguarding concerns directly.

In **supported living**, you work alongside adults with learning disabilities or mental health needs in shared houses, supporting them with daily living skills, community activities and personal care, often working shifts rather than short timed visits.

### Why the UK cannot fill these roles domestically

The care sector in England, Scotland and Wales faces a structural workforce shortage that has persisted for over a decade. The reasons are specific: the pay is low relative to the physical and emotional demands, working hours are unsocial, progression is slow without significant further training, and the work carries little social prestige despite being genuinely skilled. The Care Quality Commission and sector bodies have reported vacancy rates in adult social care running into the hundreds of thousands. This is not a temporary blip — it reflects demographic reality, with an ageing population driving demand upwards at the same time as domestic labour supply tightens. That is why the Home Office added care roles to the eligible occupation list and why employers hold sponsor licences specifically to recruit from overseas.

## What Care Assistants Are Paid in the UK

Salaries for care assistants are quoted in several ways, which causes confusion. Many employers advertise an **hourly rate** — typically somewhere between £11 and £13 per hour for standard hours in 2026, though regional variation is significant. London and the South East generally pay more, but living costs there are also substantially higher. Rural areas may pay less but offer lower rent.

Annualised, a full-time care assistant working 37–40 hours per week earns indicatively between £20,000 and £27,000 gross per year. Weekend and bank holiday enhancements, night-shift premiums and overtime can push earnings higher, and some experienced senior care assistants or those with additional responsibilities (lead carer, medication-trained, dementia specialist) earn above the midpoint of that range.

What this means for visa purposes is important: the employer must pay you at least the higher of the general Skilled Worker salary threshold and the going rate for the specific occupation code. Both figures are set by the Home Office and **change periodically**, so you must verify the current thresholds on GOV.UK before assessing whether a job offer qualifies. Never accept a recruiter's assurance that a salary is compliant — check the official source yourself.

| Factor | Effect on salary |
|---|---|
| Employer type (NHS trust vs. private care home vs. small domiciliary provider) | NHS pay bands are structured; private rates vary widely |
| Region | London and South East higher; North and Midlands lower |
| Shift pattern (nights, weekends, bank holidays) | Enhancements of 25–50% on base rate are common |
| Experience and qualifications | Level 3 diploma or medication training may attract higher banding |
| Full-time vs. part-time | Annualised salary must still meet threshold at full-time equivalent |
| Live-in care | Some employers deduct accommodation from gross pay — verify how this interacts with threshold compliance |

## The Visa Route in Detail

### The Health and Care Worker sub-route

The relevant route is the **Skilled Worker visa — Health and Care Worker sub-route**. It was introduced specifically to streamline recruitment of overseas health and care workers and carries a lower application fee than the standard Skilled Worker visa, plus an exemption from the Immigration Health Surcharge (verify this remains current on GOV.UK). The SOC (Standard Occupational Classification) code most commonly applied to care assistant roles is **6145 — Care Workers and Home Carers**, though employers should confirm the correct code for the specific role.

### What the employer must do first

The process is always employer-led. The employer must:

1. Hold a valid **Home Office sponsor licence** (check the published register on GOV.UK).
2. Carry out a **Resident Labour Market Test** if required — though care roles on the shortage occupation framework have had modified requirements. Verify current rules on GOV.UK.
3. Assess whether the role and salary genuinely meet the eligible occupation code and threshold.
4. Issue a **Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS)** — a unique reference number assigned to you, confirming the job details.

Without a CoS, you cannot make a visa application. No agent or intermediary can obtain a CoS independently — it must come directly from a licensed employer who has offered you a genuine job.

### What you must provide

Once you hold a CoS reference number, you apply online for the visa and submit supporting documents. These typically include:

- Valid passport or travel document
- Certificate of Sponsorship reference number
- Proof of English language ability (approved SELT such as IELTS for UKVI, or qualifying degree taught in English)
- Evidence of finances (you must show you can support yourself unless your sponsor certifies they will do so)
- Tuberculosis test results if you are from a listed country
- Criminal record certificate if required for the care role (many employers require a Disclosure and Barring Service check equivalent from your home country)

### Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS)

Because care work involves vulnerable adults, UK employers require an **Enhanced DBS check** before or shortly after you start. For overseas applicants, employers typically also request a certificate of good conduct or police clearance from your home country. Arrange this early — in some countries it takes weeks to obtain.

## Qualifications and Registration

Unlike nursing or social work, the care assistant role does not require statutory registration with a professional body as a condition of employment in England. However, **Wales** requires care workers to register with **Social Care Wales**, and **Scotland** requires registration with the **Scottish Social Services Council (SSSC)**. If you intend to work in Wales or Scotland, factor in the registration timeline — it is not instant and requires evidence of qualifications and references.

In England, the **Care Certificate** — a set of 15 standards covering areas such as safeguarding, infection control, communication and person-centred care — is the expected baseline. Most employers complete this with new starters during induction rather than requiring it before employment. Overseas applicants who have worked in care internationally may find some of their prior learning mapped against the standards, but the formal certificate must be assessed in a UK context.

A **Level 2 or Level 3 Diploma in Health and Social Care** (awarded by bodies such as NCFE, CACHE or City & Guilds) strengthens your application significantly and may qualify you for a higher pay banding. Some sponsors offer to fund these qualifications after a probationary period.

## Which Types of Employer Actually Sponsor Care Assistants

Not every care provider sponsors overseas workers, and not every organisation on the sponsor register is actively hiring internationally. Understanding where to look saves time.

**Large private care home groups** — companies operating 20 or more homes across England — are the most active sponsors. They have dedicated HR infrastructure, existing relationships with overseas recruitment pipelines, and sufficient volume to justify the administrative cost of maintaining a sponsor licence. Names to research include well-known national chains; search their careers pages directly for international recruitment sections.

**NHS trusts and NHS-commissioned services** — some NHS trusts employ care assistants in community and hospital settings and hold sponsor licences. NHS Jobs is the starting point.

**Local authority-commissioned home care providers** — these are private companies contracted by councils to deliver domiciliary care. Many hold licences but recruit overseas less consistently than residential providers.

**Small independent care homes** — a single-site operator with 30 beds can hold a licence and sponsor, but their capacity is limited. They may sponsor one or two overseas workers at a time. Direct approaches to local care homes in areas with known shortages can be productive.

**Avoid** anyone who is not on the published register, regardless of how convincing their job posting looks.

## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected — and How to Fix Them

1. **Applying before a Certificate of Sponsorship is issued.**
   *Fix:* Do not start the visa application until you have the CoS reference number in hand. There is no workaround.

2. **Accepting a job offer where the salary falls below the applicable threshold.**
   *Fix:* Before signing a contract, check the current going-rate threshold for SOC code 6145 on GOV.UK yourself. Do not rely on the employer's assurance.

3. **Using a general English language test instead of an approved Secure English Language Test.**
   *Fix:* Confirm the specific tests accepted for your visa category on GOV.UK before booking. Some qualifications and nationalities are exempt — check whether you qualify for an exemption.

4. **Submitting inconsistent documents** — e.g. name spelling differs between passport and qualification certificates.
   *Fix:* Review every document for consistency. Obtain a deed poll or statutory declaration if your name has changed.

5. **Failing to obtain a police clearance certificate from your home country early enough.**
   *Fix:* Start this process as soon as you have a job offer. Some countries take six to ten weeks to issue these certificates.

6. **Assuming the sponsor has done everything correctly without verifying.**
   *Fix:* Confirm the employer appears on the Home Office sponsor register before signing anything. Look up the register yourself on GOV.UK — do not accept a screenshot from the employer.

7. **Working for a sponsor before your visa is granted or switching employers without updating your visa.**
   *Fix:* You may only work for the specific sponsor named on your CoS. If you change employer, your new employer must issue a new CoS and you may need to apply for a new or updated visa.

8. **Applying through an agent who claims they can guarantee a visa.**
   *Fix:* No agent can guarantee a Home Office decision. If anyone makes this claim, they are either lying or describing something illegal. Walk away.

## Scam Warning: How to Protect Yourself

The care sector is targeted by fraudsters because overseas applicants are eager, sometimes inexperienced with UK employment law, and may be prepared to pay significant sums for what they believe is a genuine opportunity.

**No legitimate employer charges you for a job offer.** No legitimate employer charges you to process your visa. No legitimate recruitment agent charges the candidate for placing them — legitimate agencies charge the employer. If anyone asks you to pay upfront fees for "processing", "visa arrangement", "registration" or "document handling" in connection with a care job in the UK, this is a scam. Stop contact immediately.

**How to verify a sponsor:** Go directly to GOV.UK and search the Register of Licensed Sponsors. The register is publicly available and searchable by employer name. If the organisation offering you a job does not appear on that register, they cannot legally sponsor you — full stop. Be aware that some scammers use names similar to real registered organisations, so check the exact legal name and location.

**Red flags to watch for:**
- Job offered without any interview, or after only a very brief WhatsApp conversation
- Request for payment at any stage
- Salary promised far above the indicative market rate for no clear reason
- Contract sent before the employer has verified your qualifications or identity
- CoS reference number provided before a formal job offer and contract are signed

If you are unsure whether an offer is legitimate, report your concerns to the Home Office's Employer Checking Service or to Action Fraud in the UK.

## Realistic Timeline: From Application to Arrival

Understanding how long this actually takes prevents panic and poor decisions.

| Stage | Typical duration |
|---|---|
| Finding a sponsored vacancy and applying | 2–8 weeks (job boards, direct approaches) |
| Employer shortlisting, interviews, references | 2–6 weeks |
| Employer internal approval and CoS assignment | 1–3 weeks after job offer |
| Gathering your documents (police clearance, SELT results, financial evidence) | 4–10 weeks — often the longest step |
| Visa application decision (standard service) | Up to 3 weeks from valid application submission |
| Pre-arrival steps (DBS paperwork, accommodation, induction booking) | 1–3 weeks |
| **Total realistic range** | **3–6 months from first application to starting work** |

The document-gathering stage is where most applicants lose time. Book your English language test as soon as you have a realistic job prospect, not after you have a CoS — test dates fill up and results take time to arrive.
