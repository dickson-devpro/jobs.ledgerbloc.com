*Checked 11 November 2026. Occupation lists and salary thresholds change — confirm against the official source for your target country.*

The standard approach is to open a job board, filter by country, and start applying. Six months later the applicant has sent hundreds of applications, heard almost nothing, and concluded that nobody hires foreigners.

The market is not closed. It is filtered, and they were on the wrong side of the filter from day one. Nearly every rejection happens before a human reads anything, for a reason that was knowable in advance: the employer cannot sponsor, the salary cannot clear the threshold, or the occupation is not on a list it needed to be on.

## Start from your occupation

The useful question is not "where should I move" but "which countries have a route for what I do".

Occupation is the organising principle of every skilled migration system. Countries publish lists. Points systems weight occupations differently. Salary thresholds are set per occupation code. The same person can be highly employable in one country and formally ineligible in another, purely on classification.

The first hour of work: find your occupation on each country's current list and note the code. Everything follows from the code — the going rate you must beat, whether you need a skills assessment, which body assesses you, and whether your occupation sits in a priority category.

If your occupation appears on no list anywhere, that is extremely valuable to know on day one rather than month nine.

## Three systems, three kinds of work

**Employer-led.** The UK. A licensed employer must offer a specific job at or above a salary floor. Your project is a job search, and the people who succeed target licensed sponsors specifically.

**Points-based.** Australia, Canada. You apply on your own merits. Your project is a documentation exercise — skills assessment, language test, evidence of experience, police certificates.

**Cap-and-lottery.** The US H-1B. You need an employer and then luck, which means never building a plan around a single registration cycle.

Identifying which you are in tells you where effort should go. Sending CVs into a points-based system achieves nothing. Gathering documents for an employer-led system achieves nothing either.

## Where sponsorship concentrates

**Healthcare.** The most consistently sponsored category in the world, with established pipelines and sometimes employer-funded costs. The binding constraint is professional registration rather than immigration — your qualification usually has to be recognised by a regulator before you can practise, and that is separate and frequently slower.

**Engineering.** Civil, mechanical, electrical, process. Long-standing shortages, usually listed, usually requiring a skills assessment.

**Technology.** Widely sponsored, though salary floors exclude junior roles almost everywhere.

**Skilled trades.** Electricians, welders, plumbers, heavy vehicle mechanics. Consistently underrated by applicants and in real demand, particularly in regional Australia and Canada.

**Education.** Shortage subjects and higher education academics. Universities are heavy users of sponsored routes, and several of the occupation codes they use are exempt from the Immigration Skills Charge entirely.

**Seasonal and agricultural work.** Real, legal and structurally temporary. These rarely lead anywhere permanent.

## Shortlisting by what you do

A starting point for research, not a ranking.

**Healthcare** — the UK, Australia, Canada, Ireland and the Gulf. Start with the regulator, not the visa page.

**Technology** — Canada first for permanent status without a lottery, the UK for predictability if you clear £41,700, Australia if you are under 33, the US only with a willing employer or an O-1A case.

**Skilled trades** — Australia and Canada, specifically their regional programmes.

**Engineering** — almost everywhere is open, and the real work is the skills assessment.

**Teaching** — shortage subjects in the UK, international schools in the Gulf and Asia, Australia's state lists.

**Finance and professional services** — large firms with international client bases sponsor routinely, and an internal transfer is often faster than an external search.

## The filtering sequence

1. Identify your occupation code in each target country and the threshold attached.
2. Compare that threshold with the seniority you can credibly claim.
3. Check whether you need a skills assessment or professional registration, and start it now.
4. For employer-led countries, build your target list from the official sponsor register.
5. Sit your language test early. In points systems a band can be worth more than years of experience.
6. Apply narrowly and specifically, stating your position in the first line.

## Making your application legible

**CV conventions differ sharply.** The UK and US expect no photograph, no date of birth, no marital status, and a short document. Much of continental Europe expects a photograph and more personal detail. Australia tolerates a longer document with more depth per role.

**Contextualise your employers.** A company everyone at home knows is anonymous abroad. One clause — sector, size, what it does — turns a name into evidence.

**Translate your qualifications.** Grading systems do not travel.

**Lead with outcomes, not duties.** What changed because you were there, with numbers.

**Address the status question in the first line.** Which route you would need, and that you understand the employer must be licensed.

## Interviewing across time zones

**Propose times in both time zones explicitly**, with the date, because a meeting crossing midnight lands on a different day for one of you. Confirm in writing.

**Expect the status question early**, and answer plainly: which route, what the employer would need to do, roughly how long. Vagueness reads as unprepared or evasive.

**Ask about the move.** Who pays the visa fee and health charge. What relocation support exists. What happens if the role ends in the first year.

## Evaluating the offer

A salary in a foreign currency means little alone. Convert it into what it has to pay for.

**Tax.** Marginal rates, social contributions, and whether your home country also taxes you.

**Housing.** The largest single variable, differing enormously between cities a hundred miles apart.

**Healthcare.** Public, insurance-based or employer-provided. Count any immigration health charge as part of the cost of the job — £1,035 per person per year in the UK is a real number against a salary.

**Schooling**, if you have children. International school fees can exceed the entire increase that prompted the move.

**Pension or superannuation.** What the employer contributes and whether it travels with you.

**The move itself.** Visa fees and health charges for the family, shipping, rental deposits in a country where you have no credit history, flights.

Build the household's monthly budget in the destination currency and compare with what you have now. Plenty of offers that look like large increases are small ones, and some are reductions.

## The first ninety days

**Open a bank account immediately**, because nearly everything depends on it. If one bank refuses, try another — the law usually permits more than the first branch tells you.

**Get your tax number**, or many countries withhold at a penal rate.

**Register with a doctor** before you need one.

**Accept that you have no credit history.** Larger deposits, refusals on ordinary credit, and a year of building a record from a secured card or equivalent.

**Keep every immigration document together and backed up.** Entry stamps, grant letters, residence cards.

**Diarise every expiry date** — visa, passport, residence permit, follow-up requirements. The most common way people fall out of status is not a dramatic refusal; it is a date nobody was watching.

## The scams

**Never pay for a job.** Sponsorship costs are employer costs by law and cannot be recovered from the worker.

**An offer without an interview is not an offer.**

**Check the employer is real and licensed.** The UK sponsor register is public. Australia publishes information on approved sponsors. The US publishes labour condition application disclosure data.

**Watch the small signals.** Free email domains. A name almost but not quite matching a real company. Pressure to decide today. Requests for passport scans before any formal stage.

**Check the adviser.** Where immigration advice is regulated there is a public register.

Money flows from the employer to you, never the reverse.

## Remote work is not immigration

Working remotely for a foreign employer does not give you the right to live in that employer's country, and in most cases does not give you the right to work from wherever you happen to be either — paid work on a tourist visa generally breaches its conditions.

Digital nomad visas exist and are genuine options, but most do not lead to permanent residence, most require proof of income above a threshold, and many carry tax consequences people discover late.

## Where the published numbers mislead

**Country "ease of immigration" rankings** are built on a generic applicant and will not match your occupation, age or qualifications.

**Average salary figures by country** vary more by city and seniority than by border, and comparing them without adjusting for tax, housing and healthcare produces a meaningless number.

**"Most in demand" lists from recruitment firms** are marketing. Official occupation lists are published by governments and are the only ones that matter for eligibility.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which country is easiest for a work visa?**
No general answer — it depends on your occupation, age, language and qualifications.

**Can I get a job abroad without experience?**
Rarely through skilled routes, because salary thresholds exclude junior roles.

**Do I need the local language?**
For English-speaking destinations you still need to evidence English through an approved test unless your nationality exempts you.

**Should I use a recruitment agency?**
Legitimate agencies are paid by employers. One asking you for money is not.

**Will my professional licence transfer?**
Rarely automatically. Regulated professions usually require recognition by a local body.

**Can my family come?**
Usually, as dependants, with fees and often health charges per person. Check whether your route lets a partner work.

**How many applications should I send?**
Far fewer than a domestic search, if you have done the filtering.

**What is the single most common mistake?**
Starting the job search before checking whether the occupation is eligible and whether the target employers can sponsor at all.

## Being honest about the timeline

An international move takes longer than people plan for, and the parts that take longest are the ones that are nobody's priority but yours: the assessing authority's queue, a police certificate from a country you left a decade ago, a second language test you did not expect to need.

A realistic plan starts the slow items first and runs the fast items in parallel. It does not involve resigning from anything until a decision is in hand. And it assumes at least one round of something going wrong, because something usually does.

Published processing times cover only the final step. They are averages, they exclude cases sent for further checks, and they change without warning. Plan in quarters, not weeks.

## A rough order of operations

1. Identify your occupation code in each target country and note the salary threshold attached.
2. Check whether you need a skills assessment or professional registration, and start it today if you do.
3. Book a language test, and if your result sits near a band boundary, sit it again.
4. Order police certificates from every qualifying country, starting with the slowest.
5. For employer-led countries, build a target list from the official sponsor register.
6. Calculate whether your seniority can clear the salary floor. If it cannot, that is the real obstacle.
7. Apply narrowly, directly, and with your status stated in the first line.
8. Keep a single tracker with every document and its expiry date.

Nothing on that list is difficult. The difficulty is doing them in that order rather than starting at step seven, which is what almost everybody does.

## Moving the household

The part that gets planned last and causes the most stress on arrival.

**Shipping.** Sea freight is far cheaper than air and takes six to twelve weeks depending on the route. Decide what is genuinely worth shipping, because for many households the cost of moving furniture exceeds the cost of replacing it.

**Pets.** Import rules vary enormously and some countries require months of preparation — vaccinations, titre tests, quarantine. Start this before anything else if a pet is coming.

**Documents.** Carry originals of everything in hand luggage: passports, visa grant letters, birth and marriage certificates, qualification certificates, employment references, medical records, vaccination records, driving licences. Keep scans in two places.

**School places.** Admission rounds in many countries close months in advance and do not accommodate international arrivals outside them. Contact schools before you have an address.

**The first month's money.** Deposits, agency fees, furniture, a car — the outlay in the first month frequently exceeds a month's salary, and you will have no credit history to borrow against.

## One more thing worth knowing

The single highest-value habit in an international job search is checking the official sponsor register before applying to anything. It takes under a minute per employer and it removes, on average, most of the jobs you were never eligible for.

Almost nobody does it. People spend six months applying and never once check whether the companies they are writing to are permitted to hire them. The register is public, free and updated regularly, and in employer-led systems it is the difference between a search and a lottery you are not even entered in.
