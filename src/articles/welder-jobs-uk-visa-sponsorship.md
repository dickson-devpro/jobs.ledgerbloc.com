## Can Welders Get Visa Sponsorship in the UK?

Yes — welding roles are sponsorable under the UK's Skilled Worker route, and genuine demand from employers in construction, energy, manufacturing and shipbuilding means that sponsorship is a realistic prospect for experienced candidates with the right certifications. Salaries are typically quoted as an annual figure and vary significantly by process, sector and location. Sponsorship is entirely employer-initiated: you cannot apply for the visa and then look for a job; a firm offer and a Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS) from a licensed employer must come first.

---

## What the Job Actually Involves — and Why UK Employers Cannot Fill It Domestically

Welding in a UK industrial context is far more demanding than general fabrication work. The majority of sponsored roles sit in one of four sectors: offshore oil and gas, civil and structural construction (including major infrastructure projects), manufacturing of pressure vessels and pipework, and rail or shipbuilding. In each of these, the employer is not simply looking for someone who can lay a decent bead — they need a coded welder who can work to a qualified weld procedure specification (WPS) and whose output can be verified by third-party inspection.

Day-to-day, that means reading and interpreting engineering drawings and weld symbols, setting up and operating MIG, TIG or MMA plant to a procedure, completing weld inspection documentation, and working in environments that may include confined spaces, elevated platforms, or live industrial plant with strict permit-to-work regimes. In offshore roles particularly, shift patterns can be demanding — two weeks on, two weeks off rotations are common, and the work is physical and often done in poor weather.

The domestic shortage is structural, not cyclical. The UK workforce of coded welders has aged considerably, with a large cohort approaching retirement. Apprenticeship completions in welding did not keep pace with demand through the 2000s and 2010s, and the skills required to pass a coded-weld test to BS EN ISO 9606-1 (the main European standard used in the UK for fusion welding of steels) cannot be acquired quickly. Energy transition projects — offshore wind installation, hydrogen infrastructure, carbon capture — are adding new demand on top of replacement need. Major civil engineering programmes add to that pressure. Employers cannot simply wait for the domestic pipeline to fill, which is why the Home Office sponsor licence route is being used with increasing regularity.

## What Welders Are Paid in the UK

Salaries for sponsored welding roles are most often quoted annually, though some employers — particularly those using contractor or shift-based models — may quote a day rate. As a broad indicative range, coded welders in general manufacturing and construction typically earn somewhere in the region of £28,000 to £36,000 per year in the early stages of UK employment. Experienced coded welders in more demanding sectors such as oil and gas, nuclear or specialist pipework can earn considerably more — figures above £40,000 or even £45,000 are not unusual for roles requiring multiple weld process qualifications or offshore certification.

What moves pay upward:

- **Process complexity.** TIG welding, particularly in small-bore pipework or on exotic alloys such as titanium or duplex stainless, commands a premium over MIG or MMA work.
- **Coded status and the range of procedures held.** A welder qualified to multiple WPS certificates across different material groups and positions is significantly more valuable.
- **Location.** London and the South East attract London-weighted salaries, but offshore roles based out of Aberdeen can also be lucrative. Remote or difficult-access sites often attract uplift payments.
- **Sector.** Nuclear and defence welding, where traceability requirements are extremely stringent, typically sits at the top of the pay scale.

Immigration rules require that a sponsored worker is paid at least the going rate for the relevant occupation code and meets the general salary threshold — whichever figure is higher applies. Both thresholds change, and the government has adjusted them in recent years. **Always verify the current figures on GOV.UK before assessing whether a specific offer qualifies.** An employer offering a salary below the applicable threshold cannot legally issue a Certificate of Sponsorship for that role.

## The Visa Route in Detail

### The Skilled Worker Route

Welding roles are sponsored under the **Skilled Worker route**, administered by the Home Office. There is no Health and Care Worker sub-route applicable here — this is the standard Skilled Worker path.

**The employer's responsibilities come first.** To sponsor you, the employer must:

1. Hold a valid Home Office sponsor licence (or apply for one, which takes additional time).
2. Assign you a Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS) — a unique reference number confirming the role, salary, and start date.
3. Confirm the role meets the occupation code requirements and the salary thresholds applicable to that code.

**What you must provide for your visa application includes:**

- The CoS reference number.
- A valid passport or travel document.
- Proof of English language ability to the required level (a recognised English language test or, for nationals of majority English-speaking countries, exemption documentation — verify exemptions on GOV.UK).
- Evidence of funds to support yourself in the UK if your employer is not certifying maintenance.
- Overseas criminal record certificates if required by your country of origin.
- Any role-specific documentation — in the case of welders, this typically means copies of your coded-weld certificates, employment references, and evidence of your qualifications.

You apply online through the UK Visas and Immigration (UKVI) portal, pay the application fee and the Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS), then attend a biometric appointment at a Visa Application Centre in your country. Standard processing from outside the UK is generally around three weeks from biometric submission, but this is not guaranteed. Priority services can accelerate this but are not always available.

## Qualifications and Certification: The Practical Hurdle

There is no statutory licence to weld in the UK in the way there is a licence to drive an HGV — welding is not a regulated profession in the legal sense. However, the industrial reality is that almost every employer sponsoring overseas welders will require **coded-weld certification** as a non-negotiable condition of employment.

The dominant standard in UK industry is **BS EN ISO 9606-1** for the fusion welding of steels (other parts of the standard cover aluminium, copper, nickel and titanium). Certification is issued by an approved testing body — organisations such as TWI (The Welding Institute) and CSWIP-affiliated test centres are well-regarded in the UK market. If you hold overseas certification to an equivalent international standard, some employers and testing bodies will assess it against the UK/European standard.

In practice, **many overseas candidates discover their existing certification is not directly transferable.** Common gaps include:

- Certification to a national standard (rather than ISO) that does not map directly to BS EN ISO 9606.
- Tests conducted more than two years ago (which may have lapsed under the standard's continuity requirements).
- Certification in a limited range of positions or material groups that does not cover the procedures required by the UK employer.

If your certification does not satisfy the employer's WPS requirements, you will likely need to re-test. Some employers — particularly larger contractors — will arrange and fund this on arrival. Others expect you to arrive already coded. Clarify this point explicitly before accepting any offer, and get the arrangement confirmed in writing.

For offshore work, **OPITO BOSIET** (Basic Offshore Safety Induction and Emergency Training) is required for anyone working on offshore oil and gas installations. This is a course, not a qualification in the professional sense, but it is mandatory. Most employers arrange it before deployment, but it adds time and cost.

## Which Employers Actually Sponsor Welders — and How to Find Them

Not every engineering company with a welder vacancy will go through the effort and cost of sponsoring an overseas candidate. The employers most likely to do so share a common profile: they have a large enough HR function to manage compliance, they have exhausted or struggle to recruit from the domestic pool, and the role is sufficiently skilled that they cannot simply train someone from scratch in a short time.

**Types of employer most likely to sponsor:**

| Employer Type | Typical Welding Roles | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Major offshore contractors (e.g. large subsea and pipeline firms) | Coded pipework welders, structural welders | Aberdeen, Great Yarmouth, Shetland hubs; often use approved supplier lists |
| Nuclear new-build and decommissioning contractors | Coded TIG/MMA welders, coded pipework | High security vetting requirements; longer pre-employment checks |
| Rail infrastructure contractors | Structural, thermit and MIG welders | Often tied to major Network Rail or HS2 programmes |
| Pressure vessel and heat exchanger fabricators | TIG and MMA, multiple material groups | Midlands and North of England manufacturing belt |
| Shipyards and marine fabricators | MIG, MMA, flux-cored | Clyde, Belfast (note: Belfast is Northern Ireland/UK), Rosyth |
| Specialist process pipework contractors | Coded TIG, stainless and alloy steel | Petrochemical, pharmaceutical, food and beverage |

To identify genuine sponsors, check the **Home Office Register of Licensed Sponsors**, which is a publicly downloadable spreadsheet on GOV.UK. Filter for sponsor type "Worker" and search for employers by name. If a company is not on that register, they cannot legally issue a CoS, regardless of what they tell you.

Job boards including Indeed, Totaljobs, and Reed regularly carry sponsored welding vacancies — look for the phrase "Certificate of Sponsorship available" or "visa sponsorship considered". Specialist trade recruiters — those focused specifically on oil and gas, construction or engineering trades — often have relationships with employers who sponsor and can tell you directly whether a role is open to international applicants.

## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected — and the Fix

1. **Salary below threshold.** The employer has offered a salary that looks reasonable but falls below the going rate for the occupation code or the general threshold. *Fix: ask the employer to confirm in writing that the offered salary meets both thresholds, and verify those thresholds yourself on GOV.UK before you resign from your current job.*

2. **Lapsed or non-compliant weld certification.** You submit certificates that have expired under the ISO continuity rules, or that were issued to a non-recognised national standard. *Fix: check the expiry/continuity requirements of BS EN ISO 9606 before applying. If your cert has lapsed, arrange a re-test before the employer submits your CoS application.*

3. **Employer is not a licensed sponsor.** You accept an offer from a company that claims it will "sort out" sponsorship but is not on the register. *Fix: check the Home Office register before signing anything. If they are not listed, walk away.*

4. **English language evidence missing or wrong level.** You submit an English test certificate from a provider not on the UKVI's Secure English Language Tests (SELT) list, or at the wrong CEFR level. *Fix: confirm the accepted tests and minimum level on GOV.UK and book with an approved provider.*

5. **Maintenance funds not evidenced.** If your employer is not certifying that they will cover your maintenance, you need to show the required funds in your account. Applicants miss this entirely or submit a statement that does not cover the required period. *Fix: read the maintenance requirements on GOV.UK carefully and check the statement date and balance.*

6. **Assuming the CoS is the visa.** Some applicants believe the Certificate of Sponsorship itself grants entry. It does not — it is one document in a wider visa application. *Fix: treat the CoS as the starting point, not the endpoint.*

7. **Gaps in employment history not explained.** UKVI may query periods of unemployment or career breaks. *Fix: prepare a short factual explanation for any gap exceeding a few months and include supporting documentation.*

8. **Relying on an agent who charges a placement fee.** If someone is asking you to pay for a job placement or for "visa processing" as a service independent of an employer, the arrangement is either a scam or non-compliant. *Fix: see the scam warning section below.*

## Realistic Timeline: From Job Search to Arrival

The timeline below assumes you are applying from outside the UK and that the employer already holds a sponsor licence. If the employer needs to apply for a licence first, add eight to twelve weeks to the employer-side steps.

| Stage | Who Leads | Realistic Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Job search, applications, interviews | You | 4 – 12 weeks |
| Weld test / re-certification (if needed) | You / employer | 2 – 6 weeks |
| Employer assigns Certificate of Sponsorship | Employer | 1 – 3 weeks after offer confirmed |
| You gather documents (passport, certs, funds evidence, English test) | You | 2 – 4 weeks (run in parallel where possible) |
| Online visa application submitted; biometric appointment booked | You | 1 – 2 weeks |
| UKVI processing (standard, from outside UK) | UKVI | Typically ~3 weeks, not guaranteed |
| Entry clearance vignette issued; travel arranged | You | 1 – 2 weeks |
| **Total from accepted offer to arrival** | | **Approximately 3 – 6 months** |

This is a realistic minimum-to-maximum band, not a guarantee. Delays at any stage — a busy employer HR function, a weld test that needs rebooking, a biometric centre backlog — compound. Do not hand in notice at your current job until your visa is confirmed.

## Scam Warning: Protect Yourself

The combination of skilled labour shortage and overseas demand creates a target-rich environment for fraudsters. The following rules are absolute:

**No legitimate employer or recruitment agency will ever charge you a fee to apply for a job or to process a visa.** This applies everywhere in the world, but it is especially important to state clearly in this context. If anyone asks you to pay — whether they call it a "processing fee", a "visa application service", a "registration charge" or anything else — stop the conversation.

**Verify the sponsor licence yourself.** The Home Office publishes a full register of licensed sponsors on GOV.UK. It is free to download. Search for the company name. If they are not on it, they cannot sponsor you, and you should treat the offer as fraudulent until proven otherwise.

**Do not send copies of your passport or financial documents to someone you have not verified.** Scammers routinely request documents under the guise of "pre-screening" candidates. Only provide documents once you have independently confirmed the employer exists, is registered, and has made you a formal written offer.

**Be sceptical of WhatsApp-only or social-media-only recruiting.** Genuine industrial employers use formal channels — company email domains, official job boards, verifiable office addresses. An approach that begins on Facebook Messenger or WhatsApp and quickly moves to a job offer should be treated with extreme caution.

If you are uncertain about an offer's legitimacy, report it to Action Fraud (the UK's national fraud reporting service) or to the Home Office's sponsor licence abuse reporting line.

## A Note on Ireland

The title of this guide references both the UK and Ireland. It is important to be clear: the Republic of Ireland is an entirely separate jurisdiction with its own employment permit system, administered by the Department of Enterprise, Trade and Employment. Irish permits are not the same as UK Skilled Worker visas. If you are considering Ireland specifically, you will need to research the Irish employment permit routes — the Critical Skills Employment Permit and the General Employment Permit are the main routes for skilled trades — as a completely separate process. This guide covers the UK only.
