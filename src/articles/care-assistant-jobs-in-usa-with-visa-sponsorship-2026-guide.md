## Can Care Assistants Get Visa Sponsorship in the USA?

Yes — but the route is more demanding than it is in many other English-speaking countries. The United States does not have a straightforward skilled worker visa tier that sits comfortably beneath the specialty occupation threshold. Care assistant roles do not qualify for the H-1B, which requires a bachelor's degree in a relevant field. What actually works, for most internationally hired care workers, is the EB-3 employer-sponsored permanent residence pathway, which runs through the Department of Labor's PERM labor certification process before landing at USCIS. The employer carries the legal and financial weight of that process. If an employer is willing to run it, the outcome is a green card rather than a temporary permission to work — which is in some ways a stronger outcome than comparable routes in other countries, but it takes considerably longer to materialise.

## What the Work Actually Involves — and Why US Employers Are Struggling

### The Day-to-Day Reality

In the United States, care assistants work across several distinct settings: nursing homes and skilled nursing facilities (SNFs), assisted living communities, memory care units, residential group homes, and private home care agencies. The title varies — you may see "Certified Nursing Assistant," "Home Health Aide," "Personal Care Aide," or "Resident Care Assistant" — but the core duties are largely consistent: personal hygiene support (bathing, dressing, toileting), assistance with mobility and transfers, vital signs monitoring, meal assistance, documentation in care records, and emotional support to residents or clients.

In facility-based settings, care assistants typically work under the supervision of a Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN) or Registered Nurse (RN) and are responsible for a caseload of residents during each shift. Shifts are commonly 8 or 12 hours, including nights, weekends, and holidays. Physical demands are substantial — safe patient handling, repositioning, and transferring non-ambulatory residents is a daily constant. In home care, workers travel between clients' private homes, often working with a higher degree of autonomy and managing the logistical challenges of community-based care.

### Why Employers Cannot Fill These Roles Domestically

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics has consistently projected that nursing assistants and home health aides will be among the fastest-growing occupations by volume over the coming decade, driven primarily by an ageing population. The problem is not simply a lack of bodies; it is a structural mismatch. Pay for direct care workers has historically been low, physical and emotional demands are high, and turnover rates across the sector are severe — some nursing homes report annual turnover well above 50%. Rural facilities face an additional disadvantage: geographic isolation limits the local labour pool. Facilities in these areas are often the ones most willing to invest in international recruitment because they have already exhausted local options.

State Medicaid reimbursement rates affect how much facilities can pay, which in turn affects their ability to attract and retain staff. Private-pay facilities and those in higher-cost urban or suburban markets can offer better wages, and those are often the employers with the financial headroom to absorb the cost of PERM sponsorship.

## What the Job Pays

Indicative hourly rates for care assistants in the US range from approximately **$14 to $22 per hour**, depending on several factors. On an annualised basis, a full-time role typically falls somewhere between **$28,000 and $45,000**, though some specialist or senior care aide positions in high-cost states exceed this.

The table below gives a rough sense of how setting, geography, and role type affect pay. All figures are indicative and should be verified directly with employers, as they shift with local minimum wage changes, union agreements, and labour market conditions.

| Factor | Lower End | Higher End |
|---|---|---|
| **Care setting** | Rural residential/nursing home | Urban private-pay assisted living |
| **State minimum wage context** | States with $7.25–$10 floor | States with $15+ minimum wage (e.g. CA, NY, WA) |

When evaluating an offer, ask specifically about health insurance contributions, retirement plan matching, paid leave, and whether the employer covers or reimburses CNA training and examination costs — these benefits vary significantly and affect total compensation.

## The Visa Route in Detail: EB-3 and PERM

### Why EB-3, Not H-1B or H-2B

The H-1B requires a specialty occupation tied to a bachelor's degree — care assistant work does not qualify. The H-2B is a temporary non-agricultural worker visa that is capped, lottery-selected, and designed for seasonal or peak-load work rather than ongoing direct care employment. It is used occasionally in care settings but is not a sustainable route for international recruitment.

EB-3 is the realistic route. Under EB-3, care assistants typically fall into either the "Skilled Workers" sub-category (requiring at least two years of training or experience) or the "Other Workers" sub-category (unskilled or semi-skilled work requiring less than two years). Which sub-category applies depends on how the employer's job description is structured and what qualifications they require — this matters because "Other Workers" often has longer priority date backlogs.

### How the PERM Process Works

**Step 1 — PERM Labor Certification (Department of Labor).** The employer must demonstrate that no qualified US worker is available for the position. This requires conducting a formal recruitment effort — placing job advertisements, documenting applicants and why they were not selected, and filing a completed ETA Form 9089 with the DOL. The employer cannot shortcut this step. DOL processing times vary and can be subject to audit, which adds time.

**Step 2 — I-140 Immigrant Petition (USCIS).** Once PERM is certified, the employer files Form I-140 on your behalf with USCIS. This establishes your priority date — the date from which the queue for a visa number is measured. USCIS offers premium processing for I-140 in some circumstances, which can reduce the petition adjudication time.

**Step 3 — Waiting for a Visa Number.** The State Department publishes a monthly Visa Bulletin showing which priority dates are current for each preference category and country of birth. For applicants born in most countries other than India and China, EB-3 priority dates have historically been relatively current, meaning the wait between I-140 approval and the ability to proceed is shorter. For applicants born in India or China, EB-3 backlogs can be extremely long. Check the current Visa Bulletin at travel.state.gov to understand what this means for your specific situation.

**Step 4 — Consular Processing or Adjustment of Status.** Once a visa number is available, you either apply through a US consulate abroad (consular processing) or, if you are already in the US, apply to adjust your status to permanent resident (Form I-485). Consular processing involves a medical examination and interview.

The employer's immigration attorney manages the legal filings. You are responsible for providing accurate documentation: proof of identity, employment history, qualifications, and any required CNA certification.

## Qualifications, Certification, and Licensing

This is where many international applicants underestimate the complexity.

### Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) Requirement

In the United States, anyone working as a nursing assistant in a Medicare or Medicaid-certified facility must be listed on the state's Nurse Aide Registry. To appear on that registry, you must complete a state-approved CNA training programme (typically 75–150 hours, varying by state) and pass a two-part competency evaluation: a written or oral test and a clinical skills demonstration.

Some employers — particularly those actively recruiting internationally — will sponsor you on the basis of your overseas care experience and arrange for you to complete CNA training after you arrive in the US, often at their cost. This arrangement must be explicitly negotiated and documented before you leave your home country. Do not assume it is included in a sponsorship offer unless it is in writing.

If you hold a nursing or care qualification from your home country, it does not automatically transfer. There is no streamlined international recognition pathway for care assistant-level credentials in the US equivalent to, say, nursing board endorsement. If you are a qualified nurse looking to work at RN level in the United States, the pathway is different and more structured — see the [Registered Nurse Jobs in USA With Visa Sponsorship (2026 Guide)](/registered-nurse-jobs-in-usa-with-visa-sponsorship-2026-guide/) for a detailed breakdown of that route.

### State Variation Matters

CNA training hour requirements, competency test providers, and registry processes differ between states. California, New York, and Texas each have distinct requirements. If you accept a job in one state and later wish to transfer to another, you may need to apply for reciprocity or re-test. Verify current state requirements through the relevant State Board of Nursing or Department of Health before accepting a position.

### Background Checks

Federal law requires background checks for direct care workers in federally funded programmes. A serious criminal conviction — particularly for abuse, neglect, or theft — will disqualify a candidate from working in federally certified facilities. Be transparent with prospective employers from the outset.

## Which Employers Actually Sponsor Care Assistants

Not every care provider will go through the expense and time of EB-3 sponsorship. Identifying the ones that do is a practical skill.

**Large multi-site nursing home chains and assisted living operators** are the most likely sponsors. They have in-house HR departments, established relationships with immigration attorneys, and enough volume to make international recruitment a repeatable process rather than a one-off experiment. Companies operating dozens or hundreds of facilities have the infrastructure to absorb PERM costs.

**Rural and underserved area facilities** have the strongest motivation to sponsor because their local labour markets are genuinely exhausted. A rural nursing home in a state like Kansas, Mississippi, or rural Pennsylvania may be far more willing to sponsor than a well-staffed urban facility.

**Specialist memory care and dementia care providers** sometimes recruit internationally because skilled dementia care workers are in particularly short supply domestically, and the work requires temperament and training that is genuinely difficult to find.

**Healthcare staffing agencies acting as employers of record** — some agencies operate a model where they employ care workers directly and place them at client facilities, handling all immigration paperwork centrally. This can work, but scrutinise the model carefully: understand who your actual employer is, whether they are bearing all costs, and what your contractual obligations are if you leave the agency.

**How to identify genuine sponsors:** Search job boards (Indeed, LinkedIn, ZipRecruiter) with filters for "visa sponsorship" and "CNA" or "care assistant." Look specifically for phrases like "will sponsor green card," "EB-3 sponsorship available," or "immigration assistance provided." Cross-reference the employer name against the Department of Labor's PERM disclosure data, which is publicly available and shows historical sponsorship activity. An employer that has run successful PERM applications in the past is a more credible sponsor than one making the claim for the first time.

## Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected

1. **Applying to employers who list sponsorship but have no infrastructure to deliver it.** Some smaller operators mention sponsorship availability without having ever completed the process. Ask directly: "Have you run successful PERM cases before? Can I speak to your immigration attorney?" If they cannot answer clearly, treat this as a warning sign. *Fix: Verify sponsorship history through DOL PERM disclosure data.*

2. **Misrepresenting your qualifications or experience on the I-140 petition.** USCIS adjudicators review documentation carefully. Exaggerating duties, inventing credentials, or omitting relevant employment history can result in a denial and, in serious cases, a permanent bar. *Fix: Provide only accurate, verifiable documentation.*

3. **Not having CNA certification in place, or misunderstanding what the employer expects.** Some employers expect you to arrive with CNA certification; others will fund training. If this is not clarified before you commit to leaving your country, you may arrive unable to work lawfully. *Fix: Get the training arrangement in writing before accepting any offer.*

4. **Choosing the wrong state and failing to research cost of living against salary.** A $17/hour wage in rural South Dakota and a $17/hour wage in San Francisco represent radically different living standards. *Fix: Research rental costs, transport costs, and state income tax for the specific location before accepting.*

5. **Waiting for the employer to manage everything and missing requests for evidence (RFEs).** USCIS may issue a Request for Evidence at various stages. You need to respond promptly and accurately. *Fix: Maintain regular contact with the employer's immigration attorney and keep all personal documents current and accessible.*

6. **Using an immigration agent who claims to obtain sponsorship independently.** No agent, consultant, or intermediary can sponsor you for a US visa. Sponsorship must come from an employer with a genuine job to offer. *Fix: Walk away from any individual or company claiming to "find you a sponsor" in exchange for a fee.*

7. **Failing to account for priority date backlogs before accepting an offer.** If you are born in a country with a significant EB-3 backlog, you may wait considerably longer than you expect before a visa number becomes available. *Fix: Check the current Visa Bulletin and understand what the wait means for your country of birth before committing.*

8. **Missing the medical examination timing window.** The immigration medical examination (Form I-693 in the US) has a validity period. Completing it too early means it may expire before your case finalises. *Fix: Coordinate the timing of your medical with your employer's attorney rather than doing it independently.*

## Scam Warning: Protect Yourself

International care workers are specifically targeted by fraudulent recruitment operations. The volume of people searching for sponsored care roles creates a ready market for scammers.

**No legitimate employer or agent will ever charge you money to secure a job or a visa.** Not for application processing, not for "visa insurance," not for document preparation, not for placement fees, not for "securing your spot." If anyone asks you to pay anything — at any stage, in any form — the job is fraudulent. Stop contact immediately.

**How to verify a genuine US employer sponsor:**

- Search the employer's name in the Department of Labor's Foreign Labor Certification Data Center (flag.dol.gov), which publishes PERM application data. A genuine, experienced sponsor will appear in those records.
- Verify the employer's physical address and registration. Genuine care facilities are licensed by state health departments — you can check facility licences on state government websites.
- Ask for the name of the immigration attorney handling your case and verify that attorney's credentials at the state bar association.
- Never accept a job offer that exists only via WhatsApp, Telegram, or personal email from someone who approached you unsolicited.
- Be cautious of offers that promise unusually fast processing or guarantee visa approval — no one can guarantee visa approval.

If you are uncertain whether a job offer is genuine, contact the US Embassy or consulate in your country for guidance. You can also consult the USCIS website (uscis.gov) for official information about how sponsorship works.

## Final Considerations Before You Apply

Care assistant work in the United States is genuine, in-demand, and for the right person in the right location it can be the foundation of a long and stable career — and the EB-3 green card route, if successfully completed, offers permanent residence rather than a temporary permission. That is a meaningful long-term benefit.

But the process is slower, more legally complex, and more employer-dependent than care sponsorship routes in countries like the UK or Australia. You need an employer who has the resources and genuine commitment to see a multi-year immigration process through, and you need to understand the timeline before making any irreversible decisions.
