*Checked 11 November 2026. The sponsor register is updated regularly — search it fresh rather than relying on a saved copy.*

The hard part of a sponsored job search is not the visa application. It is identifying the small subset of employers who are both **licensed** to sponsor and **able** to do it for the role you want.

Those are two separate conditions and most applicants treat them as one. An employer can hold a licence and still be unable to sponsor you, because the salary does not clear the threshold. And an employer can be genuinely keen and still be unable, because they have no licence.

## The register

The Home Office publishes a register of licensed sponsors — public, updated regularly, listing every organisation permitted to sponsor workers along with the routes they hold and their rating.

**The exact legal name.** Businesses trade under names differing from their registered entity. A company you know by one name may be registered as a holding company with another. Search variations.

**The routes listed.** A licence for one route does not cover another. An organisation licensed only for a temporary worker route cannot sponsor you as a skilled worker — this catches people constantly in hospitality and creative industries.

**The rating.** A sponsor working through an action plan after a compliance problem may be restricted from issuing new certificates. They may want you and be unable to proceed for months.

## What the register does not tell you

**Whether they are hiring.** A licence says they can sponsor, not that there is a vacancy or a budget.

**How many certificates they have.** Sponsors hold allocations. An organisation that has used its allocation may be unable to assign another until the next period without applying for more.

**Their internal policy.** Plenty of licensed organisations have a standing rule against sponsoring below a certain grade, or only where a role has been open for a defined period. That will not appear anywhere public.

**Whether your role clears the threshold.** That is the salary arithmetic, and it is yours to do.

## Reading a job advert accurately

| What it says | What it means |
|---|---|
| "Visa sponsorship available" | Licensed and budgeted. Apply. |
| "We are a licensed sponsor" | Same, stated plainly. |
| "Sponsorship considered for exceptional candidates" | Licensed, but only to avoid losing someone they really want. |
| "Applicants must have the right to work in the UK" | No sponsorship. Not negotiable. |
| "We are unable to provide sponsorship at this time" | No. The "at this time" is politeness. |
| No mention at all | Almost certainly no. Sponsors usually say so. |

## Do the salary arithmetic first

A licensed, willing employer can still be unable to sponsor you if the salary does not clear the bar.

A sponsored skilled worker must be paid the higher of **£41,700** and the published going rate for the occupation code. A lower threshold of **£33,400** applies in certain cases outside healthcare and education.

So a sponsored-eligible role advertised at £35,000 means one of three things: it falls in a category eligible for the lower threshold, the listing is wrong, or the employer has not worked it through. Ask which, politely, early.

In Australia the equivalent is the Core Skills Income Threshold, **AUD 79,423** from 1 July 2026, alongside the market rate.

## Where sponsorship concentrates

**Routine:** healthcare and social care, higher education and research, specialist engineering, technology in larger firms, finance, professional services with international client bases.

**Rare:** retail, hospitality, small professional practices, local government, most charities, and almost any business under fifty people that has never hired internationally.

**The size effect.** Large organisations sponsor more — not because they are more welcoming, but because they have the HR infrastructure, have already paid for the licence, and hire enough people for the fixed costs to amortise. A two-hundred-person company that sponsors once a year has a process. A ten-person company does not.

**Universities are a special case.** They are heavy users of the Skilled Worker route, and several of the occupation codes they use — 2111 to 2119, 2161, 2162 and 2311, covering researchers and higher education teaching professionals — are **exempt from the Immigration Skills Charge entirely**. That makes a university one of the cheapest sponsors available for an eligible role.

## Building a target list in an afternoon

**One.** Search the register and filter to your sector and region.

**Two.** Identify your occupation code and look up its going rate. Write that number at the top of the page — it is the minimum any sponsored offer must carry.

**Three.** Cross off everything too small to have a process, in a sector that does not sponsor, or that you would not work for. You are usually left with twenty to sixty real targets.

**Four.** Visit each careers page directly. Many roles never reach the aggregators, and those that do are often weeks stale.

**Five.** Set alerts on those specific careers pages rather than a job board. You hear earlier and compete against fewer people.

This takes an afternoon and replaces months of unfocused applying.

## Approaching an unlicensed employer

You are asking a business to apply to the government, pay a fee, nominate people to hold compliance responsibilities, accept ongoing record-keeping duties and expose itself to audit — in order to hire one person.

It happens when three things are true: the role is genuinely hard to fill locally, you are clearly the right person, and someone senior enough to authorise it is personally invested. If any is missing, it will not happen.

If you make the case, make it concrete. The licence is applied for online. Outline the compliance obligations so the unknown is smaller. Point out that once licensed they can hire internationally again. And be honest about the timeline.

## Between offer and start date

**The employer assigns a Certificate of Sponsorship** — a database entry with a reference number carrying job title, occupation code, salary and dates. Check every field; errors propagate into your application.

**You apply online and pay** — visa fee and health surcharge together, up front, for the full length and every dependant.

**Biometrics** at a visa application centre, or via an app in some cases.

**Supporting documents** — passport, maintenance evidence, English evidence, qualifications if required, TB test results if applying from a listed country.

**Decision, then travel.**

The gap is weeks at best, and employers new to this routinely underestimate it. Raise a realistic start date during the offer conversation.

## Four questions before accepting

**What occupation code will the certificate use, and what is its going rate?**

**Will you cover the visa fee and health surcharge, including dependants?**

**What happens to my status if the role is made redundant in the first year?** The window to find another sponsor is short.

**Who handles sponsorship internally?** A named person who has done this before is a different proposition from someone working it out between other tasks.

## If a sponsor loses its licence while you work there

Rare, but it happens, and the consequences fall on the worker as much as the employer.

Revocation curtails the permission of every sponsored worker the organisation employs. You would normally be given a short period to find a new sponsor, switch route, or leave.

The protections are ordinary good practice: know whether your employer has been downgraded, keep your documents current and together, maintain a relationship with at least one other licensed employer in your field, and do not let your savings fall to nothing — a new application costs money at exactly the moment your income stops.

## The scams

**Never pay for a job.** Sponsorship costs are employer costs by law. Any request for money in exchange for a job offer is fraudulent.

**An offer without an interview is not an offer.**

**Check the employer against the register.** A company you cannot find there cannot hire you, however convincing the letterhead.

**Watch the signals.** Free email domains instead of a company domain. A name almost but not quite matching a real company. Pressure to decide within hours. Requests for passport scans before any formal stage.

**Check the adviser.** Immigration advice is regulated in the UK with a public register. An unregulated agent charging for advice is committing an offence.

Money flows from the employer to you, never the reverse.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if a company can sponsor me?**
Search the public register for its exact legal name.

**Can a company sponsor me if it is not on the register?**
Not immediately. It would have to apply for a licence first.

**Should I mention sponsorship in my application?**
Yes, early and plainly.

**Can I be asked to pay for sponsorship?**
No. A request for payment is a warning sign.

**What is a Certificate of Sponsorship?**
A record on a government database with a reference number, used in your visa application.

**What happens if I lose the job?**
Your permission is curtailed and you have a short window to find another licensed sponsor.

**Do recruitment agencies sponsor?**
Some do, where they employ you directly. Many do not. Ask which, early.

**Is it easier if I am already here on a student visa?**
Yes, meaningfully — the study-to-work switch exempts the employer from the Immigration Skills Charge, and you can start immediately.

## Adapting your CV for a sponsored application

Applications are read by people with local conventions, and small mismatches read as carelessness.

**Match the local convention.** The UK expects no photograph, no date of birth, no marital status, and a short document. Much of continental Europe expects a photograph and more personal detail. Australia tolerates a longer document with more depth per role.

**Contextualise your employers.** A company everyone in your country knows is anonymous abroad. One clause — the sector, the size, what the business does — turns a name into evidence.

**Translate your qualifications.** Grading systems do not travel. Give the equivalent, or describe plainly what standard you reached.

**Lead with outcomes.** What changed because you were there, with numbers where you have them.

**Make the occupation code visible.** If the role you want is coded in a way your CV does not obviously support, adjust the emphasis so the duties you describe line up with the code the employer will have to use. Not dishonestly — but if half your actual work supports the code and your CV only describes the other half, you have made their decision harder for no reason.

## Being realistic about volume

International job searches take longer than domestic ones, often much longer. You are competing against candidates who need no paperwork, no fees and no waiting period, and the employer has to want you enough to accept all three.

So the strategy is not volume. It is specificity: fewer applications, to licensed sponsors, in sectors that sponsor, for roles senior enough to clear the salary threshold, with your position stated plainly in the first line.

Fifty well-targeted applications will outperform five hundred untargeted ones, and will cost far less of the only thing you cannot replace.

## A checklist before the first application

- My occupation appears on the current list, and I know its code
- I know the salary threshold or going rate attached to that code
- The roles I am targeting plausibly pay above it
- I have checked the employer against the official sponsor register
- I have confirmed which routes their licence covers
- I have started any skills assessment or professional registration required
- I have a language test booked or completed at the band I need
- My CV follows the destination's conventions and contextualises my employers
- My first line states which route I would need

## Interviewing from another country

**Propose times in both time zones explicitly**, with the date, because a meeting crossing midnight lands on a different day for one of you. Confirm in writing.

**Expect the status question early.** Answer plainly: which route, what the employer would need to do, roughly how long it takes. Vagueness here reads as unprepared or evasive, and both are fatal.

**Ask about the move.** Who pays the visa fee and health charge. What relocation support exists. What happens if the role ends in the first year. The answers tell you how seriously the company takes international hiring — and an employer who has thought about none of it is an employer whose compliance is probably equally unconsidered.
